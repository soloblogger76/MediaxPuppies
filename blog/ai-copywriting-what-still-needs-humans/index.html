AI writes the first draft. A human still decides what wins

The draft is not the job. Here are the four tells that make AI copy underperform in a feed, and how to edit them out.

Media Puppies team · 23 August 2026 · Updated 15 October 2026

AI copywriting is genuinely good at first drafts. It is reliably bad at knowing which draft will work. Those two facts define the whole relationship, and most teams get the split wrong in one direction or the other — either refusing to use it at all, or shipping raw output and wondering why performance dipped.

The four tells of AI copywriting

AI copy has a texture. Once you can see it, you cannot unsee it, and neither can your audience.

1. Balanced rhythm

Sentences of similar length, tidy rule-of-three lists, clauses that arrive in pairs. Human writing is lumpy. Some sentences run on. Some do not.

Fix: break the pattern deliberately. Put a three-word sentence next to a long one.

2. Conclusions that summarise instead of landing

AI loves to end by restating. Ads do not have room for that, and it wastes the most valuable line you have.

Fix: delete the last sentence. Almost always an improvement.

3. Specificity that is not specific

Significantly reduce your cost per lead sounds concrete and says nothing. Real copy says ₹184 per lead, 62 a month.

Fix: every claim gets a number, or it gets cut. If you do not have the number, that is your real problem.

4. No point of view

Models are trained to be agreeable, so they write ads nobody could object to. Ads nobody objects to are also ads nobody remembers. The line that makes a reader think that is a bit strong is often the one that performs.

Fix: a human decides the opinion before generation starts, and defends it through the edit.

An ad that offends nobody usually persuades nobody either.

What AI is genuinely better at

  • Volume of angles. Twenty different reasons someone might care, in a minute. A human takes an afternoon and produces eight.
  • Rewriting for a different objection. Same offer, reframed for price-sensitive versus time-sensitive buyers, instantly.
  • Turning a transcript into usable copy. Feed it a sales call and ask for the exact phrases the customer used. This is the single most underrated use.
  • Breaking a blank page. The hardest part of writing has never been the words.

What stays human, permanently

  1. The offer. No copy fixes a weak offer. This is a business decision, not a writing one.
  2. Which test to run next. The model will justify any choice you present. Judgement is the job.
  3. Compliance. AI-written health, finance and income claims get accounts restricted. Every line goes past someone who knows the policies.
  4. Deciding what won. The numbers decide. Ask a model to interpret results and it will narrate whatever story you have implied.

The workflow that works

Human sets the angle and supplies real customer language. AI drafts at volume. Human edits for rhythm, specificity and opinion. Human ships, tests and decides. We described the full pipeline in how we ship 40 creatives a week.

The tool changed how fast you reach a draft. It did not change what makes an ad work, which is still a clear offer, a specific claim and a reason to act today.

Questions people ask

Is AI copywriting good enough for ads?

For first drafts and volume, yes. For the final decision, no. AI will produce twenty variants quickly and has no view on which one will sell, and the one it ranks highest is usually the most generic.

What are the tells of AI-written ad copy?

Abstractions where a number belongs, balanced sentences that commit to nothing, vocabulary nobody uses out loud, and a closing line that summarises rather than asks. Each one is fixable in a single editing pass.

What is AI genuinely better at?

Volume and variation: thirty headlines from one angle, rewriting for a different audience, or compressing to a character limit. Those are mechanical jobs where speed matters more than taste.

What stays human?

Choosing the angle, knowing which claim is credible for this brand, and deciding what to leave out. Those depend on knowing the customer and the business, which is exactly what the model does not have.
