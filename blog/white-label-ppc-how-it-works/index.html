White label PPC: how it works, what it costs agencies and where it goes wrong

Most white label relationships fail for the same reason: nobody wrote down where the agency stops and the partner starts.

Media Puppies team · 9 October 2026 · Updated 27 October 2026

White label PPC is an arrangement where a specialist partner runs pay-per-click campaigns for an agency's clients, under the agency's brand. The agency sells the service, sets the price and keeps the client relationship. The partner builds and manages the Google Ads, Microsoft Ads or Meta campaigns, sends data and activity notes in the agency's format, and never deals with the client directly.

White label PPC exists because of one recurring moment: an agency wins a client who then asks who will run their ads. There are four options. Turn the work down, hand it to someone junior, spend months hiring, or bring in a delivery partner. The fourth is what white label means, and it is unremarkable in every other industry. This guide explains how white label PPC works day to day, why agencies use it, how partners price it, where it goes wrong, and how to start without risking a client.

How white label PPC works, step by step

  1. Agreement first. A mutual NDA and a non-solicit clause, so the partner cannot approach your client during the work or after it ends.
  2. Access through the client's own accounts. The client adds the partner to their Google Ads account, Meta Business Manager or Microsoft Ads account as a user or partner. The accounts stay in the client's name, which protects everyone if the arrangement ends. Who owns your ad account explains why this matters.
  3. Audit and plan. The partner reviews tracking, structure, search terms, bidding and creative, and writes up what it would change and in what order.
  4. Build and run. Campaign builds, conversion tracking fixes, daily bid and budget management, negative keywords and a creative testing plan.
  5. Report under your brand. Data and a factual activity summary arrive in your template on a fixed date. You write the commentary and send it.

Where a platform shows a managing entity, it should show yours or the client's. If a client asks directly whether you use a specialist team, answer honestly. A partner that would contradict you is the wrong partner, and most clients care far more about results than about who sits at the keyboard.

Why agencies use white label PPC

Rarely capability. Usually economics and risk.

  • Fixed cost versus flexible cost. A senior media buyer is a large fixed cost whether you have three accounts or eleven. Per-account partner pricing rises and falls with your client list.
  • Cover. One in-house buyer cannot cover their own holidays or illness. A partner team can.
  • Knowledge that stays. When an in-house buyer resigns, the account history leaves with them. A partner that documents accounts keeps that knowledge in writing.
  • Speed. You can say yes to a new client this week instead of in three months.
  • Breadth. One partner can cover Google, Microsoft and Meta, which is hard to hire for in a single person.

The most common reason an agency calls a white label PPC partner is that its buyer resigned yesterday. It is always urgent and entirely predictable, which is why many agencies set up a partner before they need one.

What the agency keeps and what the partner does

The split that works best keeps everything client-facing with you and everything inside the ad accounts with the partner.

Strategy sits in the middle. The partner proposes, based on the data. You decide, because you know the client's business, margins and plans. Writing this split down at the start prevents most of the friction that appears in month four.

White label PPC versus hiring a freelancer

A freelancer is the other common way agencies cover PPC without hiring. It can work well for one or two accounts, especially when the freelancer is senior and the accounts are simple. The trade-offs appear as the client list grows.

How white label PPC is priced

Partners price white label PPC in three common ways.

Percentage of ad spendThe partner takes a share of each client's spendThe partner earns more when your client spends more
HourlyBilled against time loggedHard to price to clients and easy to dispute

A fixed monthly fee per account is usually the best fit. It lets you set your own client price with a predictable margin that does not shrink or swell with ad spend, and it keeps the partner's advice independent of the budget. White label PPC pricing covers the margin arithmetic in detail.

A worked example of the margin

Say a web design agency in Leeds has five clients who want Google Ads, each spending between 3,000 and 12,000 pounds a month. Hiring an experienced PPC specialist means a salary, benefits, tools and months of recruiting, plus the risk that the person leaves.

With a white label partner on a fixed per-account fee, the agency's cost scales with the number of accounts. It charges each client a management fee above the partner's fee and keeps the difference. If a sixth client signs, cost and revenue rise together. If one leaves, the partner cost drops the same month. The point where hiring in-house makes more sense is usually when the agency has enough PPC work to keep a senior person fully busy all year. The agency vs in-house calculator helps find that point.

Tell the client in advance that the first month is about fixing foundations, and that numbers can move around while tracking and structure change. Clients accept a settling period when they hear about it first; they do not accept it as an explanation afterwards.

Where white label PPC goes wrong

  • Scope never written down. Who produces creative? Who writes the strategy? Who answers the client at 6pm? Unspoken assumptions surface months later as resentment.
  • A partner that disappears into a ticket queue. If you cannot reach a named person who knows the account, you have bought a process, not a team.
  • Reporting that is only a dashboard. Numbers with no decisions mean you write the analysis yourself, which is most of the work you outsourced.
  • No continuity plan. Without written account notes, you have swapped one single point of failure for another.
  • Leaks to the client. Partner logos in reports, partner email addresses on invites, or forwarded emails. White label reporting covers how to stop them.

Signs your white label PPC partner is working

  • The first audit found specific problems in your client's account, with an estimated cost for each, rather than a generic checklist.
  • Reports arrive on the agreed date every month, with decisions explained, not just charts.
  • Cost per result is moving toward the target within 60 to 90 days, or the partner has explained clearly why it is not and what will change.
  • You can reach a named person who knows the account within your agreed overlap window.
  • You never have to apologise to a client for something the partner did or failed to do.

If two or more of these are missing after the first quarter, raise it directly. A good partner will want to hear it. If nothing changes, move the account; that is why the accounts stay in your client's name.

What a white label PPC report should contain

The partner's report to you is raw material, not the finished client report. It should include the month's numbers against target, the trend over 90 days, spend and pacing, every material change made and why, tests run and their results, and the plan for next month. You turn that into the client-facing report with your own commentary. The full process, including where reports tend to leak, is in white label reporting.

White label PPC for Google Ads versus Meta

The arrangement is the same on both platforms, but the work differs. Google Ads white label work is mostly keywords, search terms, bidding and Shopping feeds, and results arrive from demand that already exists. Meta work is mostly creative testing and audience strategy, and results depend heavily on how often new ads are produced. If you resell Meta, agree who makes the creative and how many new concepts arrive each month. White label Facebook ads covers the Meta side.

What to ask a white label PPC partner

  1. Who exactly works on my accounts, and can I speak to them?
  2. What is in scope and what is not, especially creative production?
  3. What happens if your buyer leaves?
  4. Will you ever contact my client? The answer should be no, in writing.
  5. What do your reports contain, and can I see a sample?
  6. What are the notice period and exit terms?

The full list, with what good answers sound like, is in questions to ask a white label partner.

Common myths about white label PPC

  • "Clients will feel deceived." Clients buy outcomes and service. Subcontracting specialist work is normal in law, accounting, construction and software. What damages trust is being caught out in a lie, which is why you agree an honest description of your team.
  • "The partner will steal the client." A non-solicit clause, client-owned accounts and a partner whose business model depends on agencies make this rare. Check how the partner finds its own clients before you sign.
  • "Quality drops when you outsource." It drops when nobody checks the work. Read the audit, read the monthly notes and look at the accounts yourself now and then.
  • "It only suits small agencies." Large agencies use white label partners for overflow, for platforms they do not cover in-house, and for out-of-hours monitoring.

How to start white label PPC safely

Do not start with a framework agreement covering every client. Start with one live account you already understand well, one written audit and one month of management. You will learn more from reading a partner's first audit of an account you know inside out than from any pitch, because you can check every claim.

If the audit tells you things you already knew, the partner can read an account. If it tells you something you did not know, you have found a partner. If it is generic, you have saved yourself a year.

White label PPC for agencies with no PPC clients yet

Some agencies want to add PPC before a client asks for it. That works, with a few cautions. Do not promise results you have not seen a partner deliver. Start by offering a paid audit or a short pilot to one existing client who already trusts you, priced so a disappointing first month does not damage the relationship. Learn enough about the work to explain it: what a search terms report is, why conversion tracking comes first, how long bidding takes to settle. Clients forgive an agency that is still learning a service; they do not forgive one that pretended to be expert.

Once the first account is running well, use its results, with the client's permission, to sell the service to others. Most agencies find that PPC sells most easily to clients who already buy websites or SEO from them, because the case for paid traffic to a page they built is simple to make.

White label PPC for agencies in the US, UK and Australia

Many white label PPC partners work offshore, which keeps costs down but makes time zones part of the deal. Agree a fixed overlap window each working day for questions and urgent changes. India overlaps well with UK mornings, Australian afternoons and the start or end of the US working day. Contracts should be in your currency, and data handling should meet the privacy rules your clients operate under, such as UK GDPR or US state privacy laws.

Questions people ask

What is white label PPC?

A specialist partner runs Google Ads, Microsoft Ads or Meta campaigns for an agency's clients under the agency's brand. The agency sells the service, sets the price and keeps the client.

Is white label PPC legal and ethical?

Yes. Agencies in every industry use subcontractors. It becomes a problem only if you lie to a client who asks directly, so agree in advance how you describe your delivery team.

How long does it take to start white label PPC?

Usually one to two weeks: an NDA, access to the client's accounts, an audit, then the first changes. Judge the partner on one account for a month before moving more.

Will the client know we are using a white label PPC partner?

Not unless you tell them. Reporting carries your brand, communication goes through you, and the delivery team does not contact your client. That is the arrangement, and a partner who breaks it is not a partner.

Who owns the ad accounts in a white label arrangement?

The client should, with your agency holding access and the delivery partner working inside it. That keeps the relationship yours and means nothing structural changes if you switch partners.

How is white label PPC priced?

Usually a flat fee per account, scaled by complexity and spend level, which you mark up to your own client. Per-lead and revenue-share arrangements exist and create the same incentive problems they create anywhere else.

What is the biggest risk?

Communication lag. Two layers between the client and whoever is in the account slows every decision. The fix is agreeing response times and giving the delivery team direct read access to whatever records outcomes.
