The weekly marketing report that actually gets read

If your weekly report is a dashboard link, you are asking a busy person to do the analysis you were paid for.

Media Puppies · 10 October 2026

Most marketing reports fail for the same reason: they describe what happened and leave the reader to work out what it means. A report that requires interpretation does not get read twice.

Here is the structure we use, and it works the same whether an agency writes it or you do.

1. The headline number, against the baseline you agreed

One metric. Cost per customer, cost per qualified opportunity, blended efficiency — whatever actually decides whether the business is winning. Compared to the baseline written down at the start, not to last week.

Not impressions. Not reach. Not a ROAS figure you know is double-counted across platforms.

2. What you changed and why

Every meaningful change, the reason for it, and what you expected it to do. This is the section that makes the report accountable, and it is the section most agency reports leave out entirely.

It is also the most useful thing to look back at. Six months later, a decision log tells you which of your instincts were right.

3. What happened that you did not expect

Including the bad things. A report where everything always went to plan is a report nobody is reading carefully — and it is the clearest early sign that whoever writes it has stopped paying attention.

The fastest way to lose trust in a weekly report is for the client to discover a problem you did not mention.

4. What is running now

Live tests, where each one is against the sample size it needs, and when it will be called. So nobody is surprised by a decision, and nobody is waiting on one without knowing it.

If a test cannot reach a conclusion at your volume, say so rather than letting it run forever. Our sample size calculator settles that before the test starts.

5. What you need from someone else

Creative, a decision, CRM data, a landing page fix. Listed explicitly. An engagement blocked on something nobody asked for clearly is a wasted month, and this one line prevents most of them.

Formats that guarantee it is ignored

  • A dashboard link. Dashboards are for looking things up, not for being told what matters.
  • A PDF with forty charts. Volume is not rigour.
  • A different structure each week. Consistency is what lets a reader skim to the part they care about.
  • Leading with reach when conversions fell. Everyone notices.

Keep a live dashboard anyway

Build one in a workspace you own, so nobody waits for a number. It exists alongside the written report, not instead of it. The report is the interpretation; the dashboard is the raw material.

One rule worth imposing on yourself

The headline metric is chosen at the start and does not change because a different one started looking better. If it ever needs to change, the reason goes in writing and both sides agree. That single rule prevents most reporting dishonesty, including the accidental kind.
