PPC case studies: we read 114 from 30 agencies and checked what they leave out

We coded 114 PPC case studies from agencies in the US, UK and Australia. Most named the client and gave a time window. Few gave a starting number, and fewer still said what was spent.

Every agency shortlist ends with the same step: you open their PPC case studies and look for proof. We wanted to know how much proof those pages hold. On 20 November 2026 we read 114 PPC case studies from 30 agencies in the US, the UK and Australia and recorded what each one states and what it leaves out.

The short answer: most PPC case studies tell you who the client was and roughly when. Few tell you where the account started or what was spent. 23% give a starting number for any result, 18% give the ad spend, and only 5 of 114 give a starting number, a time window and the spend together.

We wrote a guide to how to evaluate agency case studies a while ago, built around four questions: what was the baseline, over what period, what else changed, and which number is being reported. This study counts how often published case studies answer those questions on the page. We run PPC accounts ourselves, so we have a view, and we label it where it appears. Our own clients and case studies are not in this data.

What PPC case studies state, item by item

We checked eight things on every page. The client's name was there most often, at 82%. A time window came next at 68%. Everything a reader needs to size the result came much lower.

We expected the client name to be the weak spot, since many clients do not want to be named. It was the strongest. Only 21 case studies (18%) were anonymous, and 11 of those showed account screenshots instead, which is a fair trade. The real gap is the numbers around the headline.

Percentages without a starting point

68 of the 114 case studies (60%) lead with a relative claim: a % rise, a % fall or an "x" multiple. 49 of those 68 (72%) never give the number it moved from. A 300% rise in leads is a different story at 3 leads a month than at 300, and the page does not let you tell which.

37 case studies lead with an absolute figure instead: a revenue total, a lead count or a ROAS level. Those are easier to read, but they still need the spend beside them. 13 of the 37 (35%) give it. A revenue total with no spend tells you the account was big. It does not tell you it was profitable.

9 case studies (8%) had no result number at all. They described the work, the challenge and the approach, and stopped there. In a few more, the counters on the page were filled in by a script, so a reader without it (or a search engine) sees zeros.

Which number a Google Ads case study leads with

Leads or conversions led 25% of the case studies, revenue 19%, ROAS or ROI 15%, and cost per lead or sale 13%. Conversion rate led 6% and click-through rate 2%.

Two patterns stood out. First, 5 case studies filed under PPC led with an SEO or traffic number, such as organic users or keyword rankings, and mentioned the paid results lower down or not at all. Those were all multi-channel engagements. Second, ROAS was rarely labelled with its source. One UK agency called it "reported" ROAS and said how it was counted, with the words "Conversion-time reporting. Rounded figures." Our view is that every ROAS claim should carry that kind of label, because platform ROAS and the money in the bank drift apart. Our case study guide explains why.

Time windows: short, and often missing

78 case studies (68%) said how long the result took or covered. The median window was 6 months. 24 of those 78 (31%) covered 3 months or less, and the shortest was a single month. 36 case studies (32%) gave no window at all.

A month can be real. Some of the clearest pages in the sample were one-month snapshots that gave the dates, the daily budget, the lead count and a screenshot of the account. A short window with all of that is more useful than a two-year story with none of it. What a short window cannot show is whether the result held. Ask for the same numbers six months later.

When the work ran is a separate question, and 37% answered it. Undated case studies age badly. Google Ads ten years ago and Google Ads now are different products, and a page with no date gives you no way to know which one you are reading about.

UK case studies were the most likely to give a starting number (33%) and a window. US pages leaned on client quotes (47%) and showed the most screenshots. Australian pages sat in between on most items. The groups are small, 9 to 12 agencies each, so treat a gap of a few points as noise.

What the better PPC case studies do

About one case study in eight scored well. 15 (13%) stated at least four of our five checks: a starting number, a window, the spend, account data on the page, and when the work ran. They shared some habits.

  • They state the comparison. One Australian agency opens its Google Ads page by saying the results "are taken from each client's Google Ads account", then gives the date range and the prior period it compares with.
  • They give spend beside revenue. A UK agency put attributed revenue, ad spend and the resulting ROAS side by side for a named six-month window.
  • They show the account. A US agency posted screenshots of the campaign table, with the conversions and cost per conversion visible, next to each lead count.
  • They say what is not shown. The best pages noted which figures were left out and why, such as a budget that was missing from one account's screenshot.

None of this needs a bigger client or a better result. It needs the agency to publish the numbers it already has.

How to read a PPC case study (our view)

This is our opinion, built from the gaps above. Use it on any agency's PPC case studies, ours included.

  1. Find the starting number. If the headline is a % change, look for what it changed from. If it is not on the page, ask for it. 72% of the % claims we read left it out.
  2. Find the spend. Revenue, leads and ROAS mean little without it. Ask for the monthly spend across the window, even as a range.
  3. Check the window and the date. How long, and when? Ask whether the result held in the months after.
  4. Ask which channel made the number. In multi-channel case studies, separate the paid search result from SEO, email and price changes.
  5. Ask where the number came from. Platform ROAS, analytics revenue and the client's own sales figures are three different things. A screenshot of the account is good. A note from the client's finance team is better.
  6. Ask for a reference. A named client with a quote is a start. A call with that client is the real test.
  7. Weigh an audit of your own account above any of it. How an agency reads your numbers tells you more than how it presents someone else's. Our free ad account audit is one way to see that before you commit.

What we would change

Our view, as a team that runs PPC accounts: an agency case study page should carry four numbers before it carries a single adjective. Where the account started, what was spent, how long it took, and when it happened. Every agency has those figures in its own reports. Most of the pages we read led with a percentage instead.

For agencies that resell PPC to their own clients, this matters twice, because your client will ask you the same questions. If you are comparing partners, our post on white label PPC providers compared covers what they publish, and our white label PPC service reports in your own template.

We started from the 43 agencies in our earlier study of PPC agency pricing and service pages: 14 in the US, 14 in the UK and 15 in Australia, found through web searches in result order. On 20 November 2026 we looked for each agency's case study, results or portfolio pages through its site links and XML sitemaps. 39 had such a page and 4 did not. 9 of the 39 showed only SEO, web or design work.

From the other 30 we took up to 5 PPC case studies each, in the order the agency lists them, preferring those whose title names PPC, Google Ads, paid search or Shopping. Where several case studies share one page, each block counts once. One agency publishes its case studies as PDFs, and we read the PDF text.

Each case study was coded by hand from the saved text: the first result number it leads with, whether that is a % change or an absolute figure, and whether the page states a starting value, a time window, the ad spend or budget, the client's name, a dated period of work, and a quote from the client. Charts and screenshots were counted from image names, alt text and captions, so that share is a floor. Anything a page did not say was recorded as not stated.

Limits. One person coded every page, in one pass. Taking 5 per agency means agencies with dozens of case studies count the same as agencies with one. The sample leans toward small-business agencies, because it came from searches for published prices. A case study is marketing: a stated baseline does not make a claim true, and a missing one does not make it false. Agency names are in the data file only.

Questions about PPC case studies

What should a PPC case study include?

In our view: the starting number, the result, the time window, the ad spend, when the work ran, and where the figures came from. In 114 PPC case studies we read, 23% gave a starting number, 18% the spend and 68% a window. Only 5 gave all three of baseline, window and spend.

Are agency PPC case studies reliable?

They are selected wins, so treat them as examples. The useful question is whether a case study has enough detail to check. 72% of the case studies leading with a % change did not say what it changed from, which makes the claim hard to judge.

What is a good result in a Google Ads case study?

There is no single number, because it depends on the starting point and the spend. Leads or conversions were the most common headline (25%), then revenue (19%) and ROAS or ROI (15%). Compare like with like and ask for the baseline.

Should I ask an agency for the client's contact?

Yes, for at least one case study. 82% of the case studies we read named the client and 33% quoted them, so a reference call is a reasonable next step. Our guide to evaluating agency case studies covers what to ask.

How long should a PPC case study cover?

Long enough to show the result held. The median window in our sample was 6 months, and 31% of the stated windows were 3 months or less. Ask what happened in the months after.
