Hiring a marketing agency as a small business: what it should look like

Most agency advice is written for companies with a marketing team. If you are the marketing team, the rules change, and so does what a good agency looks like.

Media Puppies team · 17 October 2026

Searching for a marketing agency for small businesses turns up two kinds of result: large agencies with a "small business package", and one-person shops that will take anything. Neither is automatically wrong. But a small business buys agency time differently from a brand with a marketing department, and the engagement has to be shaped around that or it quietly fails.

Here is how the good versions work, from the side of the agency that has run a lot of them.

What a marketing agency for small businesses actually has to do

A brand manager hires an agency to execute a plan that already exists. A small business owner hires an agency to tell them what the plan should be, execute it, and explain the results in words they can act on between customer calls. That is three jobs, and the third one is the one most agencies skip.

The honest version of the job starts with a question the owner can answer: what is one customer worth to you? Everything downstream, from which channel to use to how much to spend, comes from that number. An agency that starts with a channel ("you need Instagram") rather than with customer value is selling what it has, not what you need.

The three shapes a small-business engagement takes

One channel, done properly. For a clinic, a local service business or a single-product store, one well-run channel beats four half-run ones. Usually that is Google search for businesses people look for, and Meta for businesses people have to be shown. The agency's job is to pick, commit, and resist adding channels until the first one is profitable.

A fixed scope with a monthly review. Ads, a landing page, tracking, and a thirty-minute call each month that goes through spend, enquiries, cost per enquiry and the next change. No decks. The owner should leave that call knowing exactly one number and one decision.

A pilot before a retainer. A defined ninety-day run with a target agreed up front, after which both sides decide. Small businesses cannot afford a year of "building the brand"; the pilot structure forces the agency to show something measurable early. We wrote up how to set one up in the agency pilot project.

What to avoid

Packages. "Silver, Gold, Platinum" pricing means the work was designed before anyone looked at your business. The question is not which tier you can afford but what your customer value supports.

Reports built from vanity numbers. Reach, impressions and followers are what an agency reports when it cannot report enquiries. For a small business the only numbers that matter are leads or sales, what each one cost, and whether that cost leaves a margin. These are the metrics worth tracking.

Long contracts. Twelve-month lock-ins protect the agency from the consequences of its own work. Month-to-month after a pilot is the normal arrangement among agencies that expect to be judged on results.

Being told yes to everything. A small business has a budget that cannot absorb a bad quarter. An agency that never says "not yet" or "fix the website first" is not being helpful; it is being paid.

When a small business should not hire an agency yet

There are three situations where the right answer is to wait. The offer is not proven, so nobody knows if people want it at the price. The ad budget is too small for the platforms to learn from, so the account never leaves the learning phase and every result is noise. Or the follow-up does not exist: nobody answers the phone within an hour, and leads go cold before anyone calls.

In each of those cases paid marketing amplifies a problem rather than solving it. We have a longer checklist on whether your business is ready for paid ads, and we go through it on the first call with every small business that approaches us, because taking the fee anyway is how agencies lose clients and reputations.

How to compare two agencies as a small business

Ask each the same four things. Who personally runs the account and how many other accounts do they have. What happens in the first ninety days, as a list with dates. What would make them tell you to stop. And what one number they would report every month. The answers separate agencies that have done this before from agencies that have a package.

Questions people ask before hiring

Does a small business need a marketing agency or a freelancer?

A freelancer is fine when you need one skill, such as someone to run Google Ads. An agency makes sense when the work spans ads, a landing page, tracking and follow-up, and you want one party accountable for the whole chain. The deciding factor is usually whether anyone in-house can own the parts a freelancer does not.

How much should a small business spend on marketing?

It depends on what one customer is worth and how much margin each sale leaves. An agency should work that out with you on the first call rather than quote a package. There is a level of ad spend below which the platforms cannot learn, and a good agency will tell you if you are under it.

How long before results show?

Paid search can produce enquiries within days if the offer and landing page are sound. Meta usually needs a few weeks to leave the learning phase and settle on a stable cost per lead. SEO takes months. A ninety-day pilot with a target agreed up front is the fairest way to judge.

What should the monthly report contain?

Spend, number of enquiries or sales, cost per enquiry, and the one change planned for next month. If a report leads with reach and impressions instead, ask why.
