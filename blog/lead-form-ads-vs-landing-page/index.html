Instant forms or a landing page? The honest comparison

Instant forms are cheaper per lead and usually worse per customer. Here is when that trade is worth making.

Media Puppies · 6 September 2026 · Updated 11 October 2026

Instant forms fill themselves from the user's profile. Two taps, no page load, no typing. Cost per lead collapses — often by half.

Then the leads don't answer the phone, and everyone concludes the platform is broken.

What's actually happening

Friction filters. A landing page asks someone to load a page, read it, and type their number. Most people who do that have decided something. An instant form asks for almost nothing, so you capture people who felt mild curiosity two seconds ago.

Neither is wrong. You're choosing where to put the filter — before the lead or after it, in your sales team's day.

How to make instant forms work

  • Use the higher-intent form option. Both platforms offer a version that adds a review step. It raises cost per lead and roughly halves the junk.
  • Add one typed question. A single field they must actually write removes most accidental submissions.
  • Add a qualifying question with a disqualifying option. Budget ranges where one option is honestly below your minimum.
  • Feed qualified-lead status back so the platform stops finding tappers.

Cheap leads aren't cheaper. They move the cost from your ad account to your sales team, where nobody measures it.

The test worth running

Run both for three weeks with the same creative and budget. Compare on cost per qualified lead and, if you can, cost per customer. In our accounts the landing page wins that comparison more often than not — but not always, and the exception is usually a business with spare calling capacity.

Speed to first contact decides it

Whatever format you choose, the variable that moves close rate most is how fast someone follows up. Instant forms make this worse rather than better, because the lead is colder and the window shorter — they tapped twice while scrolling and have already moved on.

If you cannot respond inside roughly fifteen minutes during business hours, the cheaper cost per lead from an instant form is an illusion. You are buying more leads and converting a smaller share of them, and the arithmetic usually lands somewhere worse than where you started.

Before switching format, check whether the actual problem is response time. It very often is.

Run both, properly

The comparison is not a permanent decision. Run each as its own campaign, with its own budget, for at least three weeks, and judge them on the same downstream metric — not cost per lead, but cost per qualified conversation.

Those proportions are typical of what we see in considered-purchase categories. The instant form wins the metric on the dashboard and loses the one that matters. In high-volume, low-consideration categories the table often flips — which is exactly why you run it rather than assume it.

A hybrid that works

Use the instant form to start and the landing page to finish. The form collects name and number with minimal friction, and the confirmation screen — a screen most advertisers leave empty — sends them to your page with a clear next step: book a slot, see pricing, view the work.

The people who continue are self-selected and worth calling first. You have effectively moved the filter to after the lead, without losing the people who would never have loaded a page in the first place.

Common questions

Do instant-form leads know they contacted me? Often not. The profile pre-fill means they typed nothing, so open the call by naming the ad and the offer rather than assuming recall.

Can I add qualifying questions to an instant form? Yes, and you should — at least one that must be typed. It costs volume and removes most accidental submissions.

Which is better for retargeting? The landing page, decisively. It gives you a pixel event and a visitor to build audiences from; an instant form gives you neither.

This gap is exactly where pay-per-lead arrangements go wrong, because the cheapest way to produce a form fill is rarely the best way to produce a customer.
