A technical SEO checklist you can actually work through

Not 200 items. The checks that actually change whether Google can find, understand and rank your pages.

Technical SEO audits routinely arrive as a 200-row spreadsheet in which everything is red and nothing is prioritised. Most of it does not matter for a 40-page business site.

Here is what does, in the order worth doing it.

First: can Google see your pages at all?

  1. Check the index count. In Search Console, Pages report. If you have 40 pages and 6 are indexed, nothing else on this list matters yet.
  2. Read the exclusion reasons. Discovered – currently not indexed usually means thin or duplicate content. Crawled – currently not indexed means Google looked and was not impressed.
  3. Check robots.txt is not blocking anything important. This breaks entire sites more often than it should.
  4. Check for stray noindex tags, especially on pages moved from a staging site. A forgotten noindex is the single most common catastrophic finding in audits.
  5. Submit a sitemap and make sure every URL in it returns 200. A sitemap full of redirects and 404s wastes crawl budget and trust.

Second: does each page have one address?

  • One canonical version: http or https, www or not, trailing slash or not. Pick one, redirect the rest.
  • Self-referencing canonical tags on every page.
  • No duplicate content across URLs with parameters. Common on filtered category pages.
  • Case consistency. On case-sensitive hosting, /seo-services/ and /seo/ are different URLs, and one of them is a 404.

Third: speed, on the device people use

Test on a mid-range Android on 4G, not a laptop on office wifi. The usual offenders are unoptimised hero images, too many font families and a head full of analytics tags. Detail in Core Web Vitals on Indian networks.

Fourth: structure Google can follow

  • Every important page reachable in three clicks from the homepage.
  • Internal links with descriptive anchor text. Not click here. This is free and consistently under-used.
  • One H1 per page, describing that page specifically.
  • No orphan pages. If nothing links to it, it will not rank.
  • Breadcrumbs on anything deeper than one level.

Most technical SEO problems are not exotic. They are a noindex tag, a blocked folder, or a page nothing links to.

Fifth: schema that earns something

Add the types that can change how your result looks:

  • LocalBusiness if you have a catchment area, with details matching your Business Profile exactly.
  • FAQPage on pages with genuine questions.
  • Product with price and availability for ecommerce.
  • Article on blog posts.
  • BreadcrumbList so results show a path instead of a URL.

Validate it. Broken schema is worse than no schema.

What to ignore

  • Keyword density. Not a thing.
  • Meta keywords tag. Unused for over a decade.
  • Chasing a perfect audit score. Tools flag issues by pattern, not by impact.
  • Minor heading hierarchy warnings on pages that read fine to a human.

We ran this checklist on our own site

On 19 November 2026 we pointed a short Python script at mediapuppies.com and ran the checks above against every live blog post. The script reads the blog sitemap and downloads each of the 268 posts. For each one it checks the title and meta description length, whether the canonical points to the page itself, how many H1 tags there are, whether a robots noindex is set, whether og:image is present, whether every JSON-LD block parses, whether every image has an alt attribute and how many words sit inside the article. It then collects every internal link and image the posts point to, 774 unique targets in all, and requests each one to confirm it returns 200.

Two days earlier, on 17 November, we ran a wider audit across the whole site: 371 HTML files, 329 sitemap URLs and 933 JSON-LD blocks. It showed the same picture. Every indexable page had a self-referencing, absolute, https canonical with a trailing slash. No internal link was broken, no title or meta description was duplicated, and none of the 933 JSON-LD blocks failed to parse.

Our view: a clean result like this is normal for a small static site built from templates. When the template is right, every page is right. When the template is wrong, every page breaks at once. That is why these checks are worth scripting and rerunning after every change to the template or build.

What Search Console showed at the same time

A clean crawl does not guarantee a clean index report. On 17 November our Search Console Pages report showed 327 pages indexed and 58 not indexed. The reasons broke down like this:

Reason in Search ConsolePagesWhat it was
Page with redirect39Old URLs we moved or removed ourselves
Excluded by noindex8Pages we keep out on purpose, such as tool embeds and form pages
Not found (404)6URLs that no longer exist
Soft 4043Removed posts that redirected to the home page
Server error (5xx)1A one-off error, sent for validation
Crawled - currently not indexed1A page Google read and chose to leave out

Most of the 58 came from cleanup we did ourselves. We had removed 15 URLs, 10 old off-topic posts and 5 city pages, and their redirects pointed at the home page or a general hub. Google tends to treat a redirect to the home page as a soft 404, because the new page does not answer what the old one did. That is where the 3 soft 404s came from.

Two fixes followed. We added 9 server-side 301 redirects in Cloudflare: 6 old URLs that used to answer with a meta refresh page, and 3 posts we merged into a stronger one. Each now returns a proper redirect. We also added an image sitemap with 263 cover image entries, one per post at the time. As the blog grew it reached 268 entries by 19 November.

The lesson for your own checklist: when you remove a page, redirect it to the closest page on the same topic. If there is none, a 404 or 410 is more honest than sending everything to the home page. Then check the Pages report a few weeks after any cleanup, as covered in our Google Search Console guide.

Where our own audit flagged the wrong thing

The section on what to ignore says tools flag issues by pattern. Our own script did exactly that, twice, and got one thing right.

Empty alt text on author avatars. Every one of the 268 posts was flagged for an image without alt text. In each case it was the small round avatar next to the byline, and that image has alt="" on purpose. The author name sits right beside it as a link, so a filled-in alt would make a screen reader announce the same name twice. An empty alt tells assistive technology to skip a decorative image, and it is the correct markup for one. A missing alt attribute is a real problem. An empty one on a decorative image is fine. Our checker treated the two the same, so the bug was in the checker. Images that carry meaning, like each post's cover, keep a descriptive alt.

Email links counted as broken. The script treated any link containing our domain as internal, so it tried to fetch the mailto: contact link as a web page and reported it broken on all 268 posts. With mailto links set aside, the broken count was zero.

Thin content, flagged correctly. Three posts came in under 600 words, and one of them was this checklist at 495 words. That flag was right, and this update is the fix. Our 17 November data supports it: posts over 2,000 words averaged 441 Search Console impressions over 90 days, while posts under 1,000 words averaged 68. Our view: length is a side effect of answering the question fully, so we added our own data here instead of padding.

The problem that only showed up after a deploy

The crawl found nothing broken, yet our images did break once that week. Right after a deploy that added new cover images, GitHub Pages returned 503 errors for the fresh image files, and those images failed to load for a few minutes. The HTML was fine and every path was correct, so an audit of the source files could never have caught it.

What we took from it:

  • Check assets on the live site after every deploy. Request a few of the newest images, stylesheets and scripts and confirm each returns 200, in addition to the HTML.
  • Recheck before you panic. A 503 that clears within minutes is a hosting delay. A 404 that stays is a wrong path.
  • Use a browser-like user agent in scripts. Some hosts and CDNs answer bots differently, and your check should see what a visitor sees.
  • Retry on 429 and 5xx responses. Our script retries those codes up to three times with a short wait, so one slow response does not show up as a broken link.

Run the same checks on your site

You do not need our script to repeat this. A basic version takes an afternoon:

  1. List every URL in your sitemap and confirm each returns 200.
  2. Record the title, meta description, canonical, H1 count and robots tag for each URL. Sort by title to spot duplicates, and see how each snippet will look with our SERP snippet preview tool.
  3. Parse every JSON-LD block. One stray quote breaks a whole block. Our schema markup guide covers which types are worth adding.
  4. Collect every internal link and image, remove duplicates, and request each once. That is much faster than checking page by page: our 268 posts pointed to only 774 unique targets.
  5. Compare the crawl with the Pages report. If the crawl is clean and the index count still lags, look at redirects and duplicate content first.
  6. Count the links pointing at each page. Our wider audit found a median of 4 in-body links per post, and 54 posts whose only in-body inbound link came from the blog index. A sound internal linking strategy is the cheapest fix on this list.

How often

Full pass twice a year. Index coverage monthly. Check after every site change, because that is when noindex tags and broken redirects appear. After each deploy, also load a few of the newest images and files on the live site.
