White label reporting: what the client should and should not see

The report is the only part of the arrangement most clients ever see. It is also where white label partnerships are most often given away by accident.

Media Puppies · 15 October 2026

White label reporting is the visible surface of an invisible arrangement. Everything else — who builds the campaigns, who writes the content, where they sit — stays behind the scenes. The report does not, which makes it both the thing clients judge you on and the thing most likely to give the arrangement away.

Worth getting deliberate about, because the accidental version leaks.

Where white label reporting leaks

In roughly this order of frequency:

  • The PDF metadata. Author field, producer, company. Exported straight from a partner's template, it carries their name even when no page does.
  • Dashboard links. A shared Looker Studio or reporting-tool URL on the partner's domain, or with their logo in a corner the screenshot cropped out.
  • Email headers. A report forwarded rather than resent, carrying the original sender in the chain.
  • Calendar invites and call recordings. The partner's personal account name on a Meet link.
  • Writing style. A report that reads nothing like the rest of your communication is noticed, even when nobody can say why.

None of these are catastrophic on their own. Together they tend to surface at the worst time — usually when a client is already unhappy about something else.

What belongs in the client report

The same things that belong in any good report, which is a shorter list than most templates assume: what changed, what it cost, what we learned, what happens next. Numbers in service of decisions rather than numbers as evidence of effort.

Specifically: cost per result against target, the trend over a period long enough to mean something, the two or three decisions taken this month and why, and what is being tested next. If a chart does not change what anybody does, it is decoration.

What stays between the agencies

Partner invoices and rate cards, obviously. Less obviously:

Internal escalations. If the partner missed a deadline and you covered it, the client does not need the story. What they need is the work, on time, and a change in the process so it does not recur.

Raw task logs. Hours worked and tickets closed measure input. Showing them invites the client to manage the input rather than the outcome, which is the wrong conversation for everybody.

Disagreements about strategy. Settle them before the report. A report that presents two views makes the client the referee of a decision they are paying you to make.

Who writes the commentary

This is the question that decides whether the arrangement works. Our view is that the partner supplies the data and a factual summary of what they did, and you write the commentary — the interpretation, the recommendation, the bit the client actually reads.

It takes more of your time, and it is the reason the client is paying you rather than finding the partner directly. It also means the person on the call can answer a follow-up question without saying they will check.

A practical setup

  1. One reporting template, owned by you, in your brand, exported from your own account.
  2. Partner delivers data and a factual activity summary by a fixed date each month.
  3. You write the commentary and the recommendation, then send it from your own address.
  4. Dashboard access, if any, on a property you own with your branding applied.
  5. Check the exported file's metadata once, when you set the template up, and then again whenever the template changes.

Our own take on what makes a report useful rather than long is on the reporting page, and the commercial terms worth settling before any of this starts are in the eight questions to ask a white label partner.

Questions people ask before hiring

How do white label reports usually get exposed?

PDF metadata carrying the partner's name, dashboard links on their domain, forwarded rather than resent emails, calendar invites from personal accounts, and commentary written in a voice that does not match your other communication.

Who should write the report commentary?

You should. The partner supplies the data and a factual summary of what was done; the interpretation and recommendation should come from the agency the client hired, because that is what the client is paying for and it means the person on the call can answer follow-ups.

What should stay between the agencies?

Partner invoices and rates, internal escalations, raw hours and task logs, and any strategic disagreement. Resolve disagreements before the report rather than making the client referee a decision they hired you to make.

What belongs in the client-facing report?

Cost per result against target, a trend over a meaningful period, the two or three decisions taken this month with reasoning, and what is being tested next. Charts that do not change anybody's behaviour are decoration.
