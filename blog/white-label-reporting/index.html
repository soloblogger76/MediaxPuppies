White label reporting: how to build white label reports clients trust

The report is the only part of the arrangement most clients ever see. It is also where white label partnerships are most often given away by accident.

Media Puppies · 15 October 2026 · Updated 27 October 2026

White label reporting is how an agency delivers performance reports to its clients, under its own brand, when a partner does some or all of the work behind them. Good white label reports show the client what changed, what it cost and what happens next, in your template, sent from your accounts, with your commentary, and with nothing in them that points back to the partner.

The report is the one part of a white label arrangement the client sees every month. That makes it the strongest part when it is done deliberately and the weakest when it is not, because a partner logo, a PDF author field or a forwarded email can expose the arrangement at exactly the wrong moment. This guide covers what belongs in white label reports, how to produce them on a fixed monthly rhythm, which white label reporting tools fit which agencies, where reports leak, and how to write commentary that keeps the relationship with you.

What white label reporting means in practice

Three parties are involved. The client hired your agency. Your partner, often a specialist PPC or SEO team, runs some of the work. Your agency sits in the middle and owns the relationship. White label reporting is the process that turns the partner's work into a report the client reads as yours.

The phrase covers two different things, and people searching for it usually mean one of them:

  • White label reports: the monthly or weekly document or dashboard the client receives, carrying your brand only.
  • White label reporting tools: software that lets an agency put its own logo, colours and domain on dashboards built from ad platform data.

The tool is the easy part. Most of what makes white label reporting work is process: who pulls the data, who writes what, who checks the file and who sends it.

What belongs in white label reports

The same things that belong in any good client report, which is a shorter list than most templates assume. A client wants to know whether the money worked, what you did about it, and what happens next. Numbers should support decisions rather than prove effort.

SectionWhat it answers
Headline resultDid we hit the cost per lead or return target this month?
TrendIs it getting better or worse over the last 90 days?
Spend and pacingDid we spend what was agreed, and where?
DecisionsWhat did we change, and why?
TestsWhat did we try, and what did we learn?
Next monthWhat will we do, and what do we need from the client?

One page of numbers and one page of commentary is enough for most accounts. If a chart does not change what anybody does, it is decoration. What stays between the agencies

Some information should never reach the client, either because it exposes the arrangement or because it starts the wrong conversation.

  • Partner invoices and rate cards. Obvious, and still the most common accidental attachment.
  • Raw task logs and hours. They measure input. Showing them invites the client to manage the input rather than the outcome.
  • Internal escalations. If the partner missed a deadline and you covered it, the client needs the work on time and a better process, not the story.
  • Disagreements about strategy. Settle them before the report. A report that presents two views makes the client referee a decision they are paying you to make.
  • Partner logins and tool names. Screenshots from a partner's tool often carry its branding in a corner.

How to produce white label reports every month

A fixed calendar stops the scramble that causes most leaks. A rhythm that works for monthly reporting:

  1. Day 1: data. The partner pulls last month's numbers from the client's ad accounts into your template. The accounts belong to the client and the template belongs to you.
  2. Day 2: factual summary. The partner writes a short, plain account of what changed, what was tested and what each test showed. No opinions, no promises.
  3. Day 3: commentary. You write the interpretation and the recommendation. This is the part the client reads.
  4. Day 4: checks. You review branding, links, attachments and file metadata before anything leaves.
  5. Day 5: send and call. The report goes from your domain, and you or your account manager walks the client through it.

For larger accounts, add a short weekly note between monthly reports: three lines on spend, results and anything unusual. Weekly notes catch problems early and make the monthly report less of a surprise. Our template for a weekly marketing report works for this.

How often to send white label reports

Monthly is the default for most paid media clients, and it is the right default. A month gives enough data for cost per result to mean something, and it gives you time to write commentary rather than paste numbers. Weekly reports suit clients spending heavily, clients in the first 90 days, and seasonal businesses in their peak weeks. Quarterly reviews suit SEO and longer B2B sales cycles, where a month of data says very little.

Whatever the rhythm, agree it in writing at the start, and send on the same day every time. Clients notice a late report far more than they notice a good one.

Setting up a white label reporting template

Build the template once, carefully, and most leaks disappear.

  1. Brand it fully. Your logo, colours and fonts, your footer, your contact details. Remove every trace of the tool or partner it was copied from.
  2. Fix the sections. Use the six sections in the table above, in the same order every month, so clients learn where to look.
  3. Name files consistently. Client name, month and your agency name, so a forwarded file still looks like yours.
  4. Set the file properties. Author, company and producer fields should show your agency. Check them by opening the exported file's properties, not by trusting the software.
  5. Lock the commentary box. Leave a clearly marked space that only your team fills in, so partner notes never ship by accident.

White label reporting tools: dashboards, PDFs or both

Three setups cover almost every agency.

Tools such as AgencyAnalytics, DashThis, Swydo and Whatagraph offer white label dashboards with your logo and domain. Whichever you choose, the account should be in your agency's name and paid by you. If the partner owns the reporting tool, they own a piece of your client relationship. The same principle applies to ad accounts, as who owns your ad account explains.

Five questions for choosing a white label reporting tool

  • Can it use your own domain or subdomain for client dashboards?
  • Does it connect to every platform your clients use, such as Google Ads, Meta, LinkedIn, GA4 and Search Console?
  • Can it send scheduled PDFs from your address?
  • Who owns the account and the data connections if your partner changes?
  • Does it let you add written commentary next to the charts?

A tool that fails the fourth question is a risk however good it looks.

Where white label reporting leaks

Reports usually give the arrangement away through details nobody thought to check. In rough order of how often they turn up:

LeakFix
PDF metadata with the partner's nameExport from your own account; check the file properties once per template change
Dashboard links on the partner's domainHost dashboards on your account or your custom domain
Forwarded emails carrying the original senderAlways send fresh from your own address
Meeting invites from a partner's personal accountCalls run from your calendar; the partner joins as a guest if needed
Commentary in a voice that is not yoursWrite the commentary yourself

None of these is fatal on its own. Together they tend to surface when a client is already unhappy about something else, which is the worst time to explain a supplier arrangement for the first time.

Who writes the commentary

This is the question that decides whether a white label arrangement works. The partner supplies the data and a factual summary of what they did. You write the commentary: the interpretation, the recommendation and the plan.

It takes more of your time, and it is the reason the client pays you rather than finding the partner directly. It also means the person on the monthly call understands the account well enough to answer a follow-up without saying they will check. A short example of the difference:

  • Partner summary: "Paused three ad groups with CPL above 1.5x target. Launched two new responsive search ads. Increased budget on the brand campaign by 10 percent."
  • Your commentary: "Leads cost more in the first half of the month because three ad groups were attracting research searches, not buyers. We paused them on the 12th, and cost per lead has been back under target since. Next month we will test a second landing page for the main service, which should lift the conversion rate further."

The second version is what the client remembers, and it is written in your voice.

Handling questions on the reporting call

The call matters more than the document. Clients rarely read every chart, but they remember whether the person on the call understood their account. Before each call, read the partner's summary in full, note the two numbers most likely to raise questions, and agree with the partner what you will say if results are behind target.

If a client asks a technical question you cannot answer on the spot, say you will confirm the detail by the end of the day, then ask the partner and reply from your own address. That is normal client service, and it keeps the conversation with you.

What to do when results are bad

Bad months are when white label reporting is tested. Resist the urge to fill the report with extra charts. Say plainly what went wrong, what caused it as far as you know, what has already been changed, and when the client should expect to see the effect. One honest paragraph keeps more clients than ten pages of supporting data.

Behind the scenes, ask the partner for a short written diagnosis before you write the commentary. If the partner's work caused the problem, that conversation happens between the agencies, and the client hears only the fix.

A worked example

Say a web design agency in Manchester has six clients on Google Ads, all run by a white label PPC partner. Each month the partner delivers data and a factual summary for every account by the 2nd. The agency's account manager writes commentary for all six on the 3rd, spending around half an hour per client, and sends reports on the 5th.

In March, one client's cost per lead rises by a third. The partner's summary flags the cause: a competitor started bidding on the client's brand name. The account manager's commentary explains it in plain terms, recommends a small budget increase on the brand campaign and asks the client for one new customer review to use in ads. The client approves both on the call. Nothing in the report or the conversation mentions a partner, and nothing needs to.

White label reports for PPC versus SEO

PPC reports move fast and are built around cost per result, so the monthly rhythm above suits them well. SEO reports move slowly. Rankings and organic traffic shift over months, so an SEO white label report should lead with trends, pages gaining and losing visibility, and the work completed, rather than month-on-month swings that mean little. Many agencies send PPC reports monthly and SEO reports quarterly, with a short monthly activity note in between.

A checklist before you send

  • Only your branding appears, including in charts, screenshots and footers.
  • The file's author and producer fields show your agency.
  • Every link points to your domain or the client's own accounts.
  • The commentary is in your voice and recommends one clear next step.
  • The email is new, from your address, with no forwarded history.

Questions people ask before hiring

What is white label reporting?

Delivering performance reports to clients under your agency's brand when a partner does some or all of the work. The client sees your template, your domain and your commentary only.

What is the best white label reporting tool?

Looker Studio suits agencies on Google Ads and GA4 who want a free dashboard. Dedicated tools such as AgencyAnalytics, DashThis, Swydo and Whatagraph add custom domains and scheduled sends. Whichever you choose, the account should be in your agency's name.

Can clients tell a report is white labelled?

Only if something leaks: partner branding, PDF metadata, dashboard links on the partner's domain or forwarded emails. A fixed checking step before every send prevents almost all of it.

How do white label reports usually get exposed?

PDF metadata carrying the partner's name, dashboard links on their domain, forwarded rather than resent emails, calendar invites from personal accounts, and commentary written in a voice that does not match your other communication.

Who should write the report commentary?

You should. The partner supplies the data and a factual summary of what was done; the interpretation and recommendation should come from the agency the client hired, because that is what the client is paying for and it means the person on the call can answer follow-ups.

What should stay between the agencies?

Partner invoices and rates, internal escalations, raw hours and task logs, and any strategic disagreement. Resolve disagreements before the report rather than making the client referee a decision they hired you to make.

What belongs in the client-facing report?

Cost per result against target, a trend over a meaningful period, the two or three decisions taken this month with reasoning, and what is being tested next. Charts that do not change anybody's behaviour are decoration.
