Running ads in multiple countries without doubling the work

A second country is not a second audience. It is a second account, a second learning phase and a second set of rules, and it needs its own budget.

Media Puppies · 11 October 2026

Running ads in multiple countries looks like a targeting change and is not. Adding a market adds a separate auction, a separate conversion dataset, a separate regulatory surface and usually a separate currency in the reporting. Treating it as a checkbox is the most common way international expansion quietly fails.

Separate campaigns, always

Do not add a country to an existing campaign. Separate campaigns per market give you independent budget control, independent bidding, clean reporting and the ability to pause one without disturbing the other. The apparent efficiency of a combined campaign is an illusion: the platform will spend disproportionately in whichever market is cheapest, which is usually not the one you most want to win.

Where markets are genuinely similar and volume in each is too low to learn from, grouping can be defensible — but do it knowingly and watch the geographic breakdown, not as a default.

Each market needs its own learning budget

This is the constraint that decides whether expansion works. A new market starts from zero conversion history, which means its own learning phase at its own cost. Splitting an existing budget across two countries can leave both below the threshold where bidding stabilises, so you end up with two mediocre accounts instead of one good one. Work out the floor per market with the learning phase calculator before deciding whether you can afford two.

The question is not whether the second market is attractive. It is whether you can fund both markets above the learning threshold at once. If not, sequence them.

What to localise and what to leave

  • Currency and price display. Non-negotiable. A price in the wrong currency kills conversion rate regardless of exchange rate.
  • Trust signals. Local phone format, local address if you have one, local payment methods, delivery times stated in local terms. These move conversion rate more than ad copy does.
  • Ad copy. Translate properly rather than machine-translating. Spelling conventions, date formats and idiom all signal whether you are a local business or a foreign one.
  • Offer and positioning. Often needs rethinking rather than translating. What is a differentiator in one market can be table stakes in another.
  • Creative assets. Frequently reusable with new copy and new price overlays, which is where most of the cost saving in multi-market work actually comes from.

The rules change with the border

Each market brings its own advertising and privacy regime. Consent requirements and the conversion loss that follows them differ — the UK and EU position is not the same as the patchwork across US states. Restricted categories differ: special ad categories, financial promotions rules and local consumer law on pricing and reviews all bite in some countries and not others. Check before launch, not after a disapproval.

Measurement in international PPC gets harder

Report per market, in local currency and in your reporting currency, and be explicit about which exchange rate you used — otherwise a market can appear to improve because the rupee moved. Keep conversion tracking separate per market so you can see conversion rate differences rather than an average that describes neither.

Time zones matter more than people expect. Dayparting, reporting cut-offs and the working window in which someone can respond to a lead all shift. The timezone overlap calculator is useful when deciding whether you can service a market at all.

Running ads in multiple countries: sequence, do not stack

Launch one new market, get it past learning, document what was different, then launch the next using what you learned. Simultaneous launches split attention and budget at exactly the moment both are most needed, and they make it impossible to tell whether a problem is the market or the execution.

We run accounts into the US, the UK, the UAE and Australia alongside India, and the recurring lesson is that the expensive part is never the media setup — it is the local trust signals and the regulatory detail. A free audit of the existing market is usually the right first step before adding another.

Questions people ask before hiring

Can I use one campaign when running ads in multiple countries?

Generally no. Separate campaigns per market give independent budgets, bidding and reporting. A combined campaign will spend disproportionately in the cheapest market, which is rarely the one you most want to win.

How much budget does a new market need?

Enough on its own to get past the learning phase, because a new market starts with no conversion history. Splitting an existing budget across two countries often leaves both below that threshold.

Do I need to translate my ads or is English enough?

It depends on the market, but spelling conventions, date formats and idiom all signal whether you are local. Even in English-speaking markets, machine-translated or unlocalised copy reads as foreign and costs conversion rate.

What usually breaks first in multi country campaigns?

Trust signals and compliance, not media. Prices in the wrong currency, unfamiliar payment methods, missing local contact details and category rules that differ by jurisdiction account for most early failures.

Should I launch several markets at once?

Sequence them. Simultaneous launches split budget and attention at the point both are most needed, and they make it impossible to tell whether a disappointing result is the market or the execution.
