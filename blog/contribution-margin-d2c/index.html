Contribution margin for D2C brands: the number to scale on, not ROAS

A campaign can show a healthy ROAS and still lose money on every order. Contribution margin is the number that tells you which one you are looking at.

Media Puppies · 18 October 2026

Contribution margin is what an order leaves behind after every cost that comes with it: the product, shipping, payment fees, returns, packaging and the advertising that produced the sale. For a D2C brand it is the clearest answer to the only question that matters about growth: does each extra order make money or lose it?

ROAS cannot answer that on its own, because it ignores everything between revenue and profit. Brands that scale on ROAS discover the gap at the end of the quarter. Brands that scale on contribution margin see it every week.

How to calculate contribution margin per order

Start with the average order value after discounts. Subtract, per order:

  • cost of goods sold;
  • shipping and packaging;
  • payment gateway and COD fees;
  • the cost of returns and RTO, averaged across all orders;
  • advertising spend divided by orders in the same period.

What remains is contribution margin per order. Multiply by orders for the period's total. Before ad spend, the same figure is sometimes called contribution margin one; after ad spend, contribution margin two. The second is the one to manage the business on.

Why contribution margin beats ROAS as a scaling signal

ROAS treats a rupee of revenue as equal whatever it cost to deliver. A discounted bestseller with high return rates and a full-price product that rarely comes back can show the same ROAS and very different contribution. ROAS also hides creeping costs: when RTO rises in a new region, ROAS barely moves while contribution falls.

The platform's reported ROAS adds a second distortion, because attribution windows and modelled conversions inflate it. Blended numbers from your own records are more honest; platform ROAS vs blended MER explains the difference.

Using contribution margin week to week

Set a floor. Decide the minimum contribution per order you will accept while acquiring new customers. It can be negative on a first order if repeat purchases reliably make it back; customer lifetime value tells you whether they do.

Scale while it holds. Increase spend in steps and watch contribution, not ROAS. When contribution per order drops towards the floor, you have found the current ceiling for that product and creative.

Read it by product and region. Blended contribution hides losers. A product line or a state with high returns can drag the whole account down while looking fine in the platform.

Turning it into a target the ad account understands

From contribution margin before ads, you can work out the most you can spend per order and still hit your floor. That becomes a break-even cost per purchase or a break-even ROAS that the media buyer can bid and budget against. Our break-even ROAS post and the calculator do that conversion.

Questions people ask before hiring

What is contribution margin for a D2C brand?

Revenue per order minus every variable cost of that order: product, shipping, packaging, payment fees, returns and the advertising that produced it. It shows whether each additional order makes or loses money.

Is contribution margin better than ROAS?

For deciding whether to scale, yes. ROAS ignores costs between revenue and profit, so two campaigns with the same ROAS can have very different contribution.

Can contribution margin be negative on the first order?

It can, if repeat purchases reliably make the customer profitable later. That decision needs a lifetime value figure you trust.

How often should I calculate contribution margin?

Weekly, by product and region where possible. Returns, RTO and shipping costs drift, and a monthly view finds problems late.
