Contribution margin for D2C brands: the number to scale on, not ROAS

A campaign can show a healthy ROAS and still lose money on every order. Contribution margin is the number that tells you which one you are looking at.

Contribution margin is what an order leaves behind after every cost that comes with it: the product, shipping, payment fees, returns, packaging and the advertising that produced the sale. For a D2C brand it is the clearest answer to the only question that matters about growth: does each extra order make money or lose it?

ROAS cannot answer that on its own, because it ignores everything between revenue and profit. Brands that scale on ROAS discover the gap at the end of the quarter. Brands that scale on contribution margin see it every week.

How to calculate contribution margin per order

Start with the average order value after discounts. Subtract, per order:

  • cost of goods sold;
  • shipping and packaging;
  • payment gateway and COD fees;
  • the cost of returns and RTO, averaged across all orders;
  • advertising spend divided by orders in the same period.

What remains is contribution margin per order. Multiply by orders for the period's total. Before ad spend, the same figure is sometimes called contribution margin one; after ad spend, contribution margin two. The second is the one to manage the business on.

Why contribution margin beats ROAS as a scaling signal

ROAS treats a rupee of revenue as equal whatever it cost to deliver. A discounted bestseller with high return rates and a full-price product that rarely comes back can show the same ROAS and very different contribution. ROAS also hides creeping costs: when RTO rises in a new region, ROAS barely moves while contribution falls.

The platform's reported ROAS adds a second distortion, because attribution windows and modelled conversions inflate it. Blended numbers from your own records are more honest; platform ROAS vs blended MER explains the difference.

Using contribution margin week to week

Set a floor. Decide the minimum contribution per order you will accept while acquiring new customers. It can be negative on a first order if repeat purchases reliably make it back; customer lifetime value tells you whether they do.

Scale while it holds. Increase spend in steps and watch contribution, not ROAS. When contribution per order drops towards the floor, you have found the current ceiling for that product and creative.

Read it by product and region. Blended contribution hides losers. A product line or a state with high returns can drag the whole account down while looking fine in the platform.

Turning it into a target the ad account understands

From contribution margin before ads, you can work out the most you can spend per order and still hit your floor. That becomes a break-even cost per purchase or a break-even ROAS that the media buyer can bid and budget against. Our break-even ROAS post and the calculator do that conversion.

How deep sale ads discount: what we counted in the Ad Library

This month we read live ads in the Meta Ad Library for two studies of our own, and both bear directly on contribution margin, because a discount comes straight off the order value at the top of the calculation. The ads ran in the US, UK, Canada and Australia. India's festive season ran over the same weeks and the Diwali set includes plenty of Indian brands, so we think the patterns travel. The Library shows no spend or sales, so these numbers describe the offers brands chose to make. They say nothing about how those offers performed.

Black Friday, read on 9 November 2026. We took the first 100 live ads for the exact phrase "black friday" in each of the four countries, in the Library's default order, and counted each distinct ad once: 209 ads from 136 advertiser pages. The full method is in our Black Friday ads study.

  • 122 of the 209 ads (58.4%) put a percentage in the main ad text. The median of the largest percentage in each of those ads was 50%.
  • 66 ads (31.6% of all 209) named 50% or more, and 19 (9.1%) named 70% or more. Only 18 (8.6%) stayed under 30%.
  • 61 ads (29.2%) used the words "up to". That is half of the ads that showed a percentage, and 37 of the 66 ads at 50% or more.
  • 29 ads (13.9%) promised free shipping or delivery, 24 (11.5%) gave a code and 11 (5.3%) ran a buy-one-get-one offer.

Diwali, read on 8 November 2026. On Diwali day we read the live ads that mention "diwali" in the same four countries: 303 distinct ads from 169 advertisers, sorted by hand into mainstream brands, Indian or South Asian brands, and ads aimed at buyers in India. The Diwali ads study has the details.

  • 68 of the 303 ads (22.4%) used sale or discount words such as "% off", "sale", "deal", "offer", "cashback", "code" or "flat off".
  • Indian and South Asian brands did so in 38 of 79 ads (48.1%), and ads aimed at India in 11 of 27 (40.7%).
  • Mainstream brands did so in 18 of 180 ads (10.0%).

Two things here matter for margin. First, the headline number is usually a ceiling. "Up to 50%" means some products carry 50% off and most carry less, so the discount that reaches your contribution margin is the average across the orders you actually ship, weighted by what sold. A brand that plans its sale around the headline figure overstates the damage; a brand whose bestseller is the 50% line understates it. Second, Indian and South Asian sellers used discount language almost five times as often as mainstream brands in the same festival. Our view: for an Indian D2C brand a festive discount is close to a default, which makes it worth costing properly every season.

What a 30% or 50% sale does to contribution margin: a worked example

The example below is illustrative. The inputs are invented for a typical-looking D2C order and do not come from any brand. The arithmetic is the formula our contribution margin calculator uses: order value minus cost of goods, shipping and packaging, and payment fees gives the margin on a delivered order. A returned order loses that margin and also costs the return handling, and the return rate spreads that loss across all orders.

Inputs: a full-price order of 1,500 rupees, 450 rupees of cost of goods, 90 rupees of shipping and packaging, payment fees of 2%, a 15% return rate and 120 rupees to handle each return. Only the discount changes.

A 30% discount takes 30% off the price and roughly half of the contribution. A 50% discount leaves less than a fifth of it: each sale order earns 147.75 rupees where a full-price order earned 772.50 rupees, so the sale has to sell about 5.2 orders to bring in what one full-price order did. Cost of goods and shipping stay the same when the price drops, which is why the damage grows faster than the discount.

The break-even ROAS row is where this reaches the ad account. A campaign holding 4x ROAS spends 375 rupees to win a 1,500 rupee order and keeps 397.50 rupees after ads. Run the same 4x on the 50% off order and the ad spend is 187.50 rupees against 147.75 rupees of contribution, a loss of about 40 rupees on every order, while the dashboard shows the same healthy ROAS. That is the gap described in why contribution margin beats ROAS, put into numbers.

Returns soften the blow slightly in this example, because a returned cheap order loses less than a returned full-price one. If your own records show the return rate rising during sales, the gap widens again; change the return rate in the calculator to see by how much.

Cost the weighted discount. List the discount on each product, weight it by last season's sale mix or your current bestsellers, and put that average into the calculator in place of the headline number.

Set a sale ROAS target per discount tier. One account-wide target will be too loose for the deep tier and too tight for the shallow one. Products at 50% off need their own floor, or their own campaign. Our festive season ads guide covers how to plan the season around that.

Count free shipping as a discount. If you normally charge for delivery, free shipping moves that cost from the customer into your margin. In the Black Friday set, roughly one ad in seven offered it.

Price the prepaid nudge separately. A prepaid discount on top of a sale discount stacks. The COD vs prepaid calculator shows whether the lower RTO on prepaid orders pays for the extra rupees off.

Our view: a deep discount can still be the right call when the goal is new customers who come back. The question then is whether the first-order loss fits inside your acquisition budget, which our post on D2C CAC in India works through. What we would avoid is running a 50% sale on the same ROAS target as the rest of the year.

Questions about contribution margin

What is contribution margin for a D2C brand?

Revenue per order minus every variable cost of that order: product, shipping, packaging, payment fees, returns and the advertising that produced it. It shows whether each additional order makes or loses money.

Is contribution margin better than ROAS?

For deciding whether to scale, yes. ROAS ignores costs between revenue and profit, so two campaigns with the same ROAS can have very different contribution.

Can contribution margin be negative on the first order?

It can, if repeat purchases reliably make the customer profitable later. That decision needs a lifetime value figure you trust.

How often should I calculate contribution margin?

Weekly, by product and region where possible. Returns, RTO and shipping costs drift, and a monthly view finds problems late.

How much does a 50% discount cut contribution margin?

It depends on your costs, because cost of goods and shipping stay the same while the price halves. In our illustrative example, a 1,500 rupee order with 450 rupees of cost of goods, 90 rupees of shipping, 2% fees and 15% returns falls from 772.50 rupees of contribution at full price to 147.75 rupees at 50% off, and break-even ROAS rises from 1.94x to 5.08x.

How deep are Black Friday discounts in ads?

In 209 live Black Friday ads we read in the Meta Ad Library on 9 November 2026 across the US, UK, Canada and Australia, 122 named a percentage and the median largest figure was 50%. Half of those 122 said "up to", so the typical discount on actual orders is lower than the headline.
