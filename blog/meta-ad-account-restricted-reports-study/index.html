Facebook ad account disabled or restricted: what 98 advertisers report about the cause, the review and the outcome

We read every public post we could find from advertisers describing a Meta restriction on their own accounts. 98 qualified. Most were still locked out when they wrote, and the few who got back in had something in common.

Key findings

  • We found 98 first-hand reports (49 on the Shopify Community, 49 on Hacker News). 10 got access back, 31 did not and 57 had no outcome yet. Of the 41 with a known outcome, 10 (24.4%) were restored.
  • The ad account was the level hit most often (42 of 98), then the personal profile (39) and Business Manager (22). 29 reports involved more than one level, and none of the 22 Business Manager restrictions was lifted.
  • 27 restrictions (27.6%) hit brand new or dormant accounts, often before a single ad ran. None of the 27 was restored.
  • 47 advertisers requested a review and 9 got access back (19.1%). 15 reached a person at Meta and 5 of them were restored (33.3%). 16 sent ID or a selfie and 1 was restored.
  • Only 20 reports said how long things took. 12 had waited a month or more, and the 2 restorations that gave a time took 10 minutes and over 6 months.

Facebook ad account disabled, and no idea why? In the 98 first-hand reports we found in public forums, 10 advertisers got access back, 31 did not and 57 were still waiting when they wrote. Of the 41 whose story had an ending, 10 (24.4%) were restored.

The level that gets restricted matters more than the wording of the notice. The ad account was hit most often, in 42 reports, but 39 involved a personal profile, and when the profile went the ads usually went with it. None of the 22 Business Manager restrictions in the sample was lifted, and none of the 27 brand new or dormant accounts got back in.

We set a target of 40 reports and found 98. They come from two forums that load without a login, so they show only what advertisers describe. Meta's side of each case is missing. We run Meta accounts for clients and label our opinions. None of our own client or account data is in here.

The sample: 98 advertisers on two public forums

On 26 November 2026 we searched the Shopify Community (42 searches, 739 topics) and Hacker News (37 comment searches and 8 post searches, 7,899 comments). We read 115 Shopify posts and 171 Hacker News comments that described a restriction, plus 38 full threads. Reddit blocked plain requests five days earlier, so we did not try it, and we did not log into Facebook.

The split is even: 49 reports from each forum, in 82 threads, posted between 2009 and 2026. The Shopify posts are mostly new stores asking for help in the same week the restriction hit. The Hacker News posts are founders and small businesses, often looking back. 12 repeat posts by the same people were merged, so each person counts once.

We expected policy breaches to dominate. They did not. Meta named a specific policy area in only a handful of reports, and the largest single group of advertisers had no idea what had happened. What surprised us most was how often the restriction landed before any ad had run.

29 of the 98 reports involved more than one level, so the rows add up to more than 98. The pattern in the table is simple. The higher up the chain the restriction sits, the less often it came back.

When the profile goes, the ads go with it

Meta ties every ad account and Business Manager to the people who run them. In 16 of the 39 personal profile reports, losing the profile also cost the advertiser an ad account, a Business Manager or a business Page. A restaurant owner lost the linked Instagram business account when the personal profile was suspended, and the card on file kept being charged for two weeks. Several store owners hit a profile restriction the moment they connected their store to Facebook for the first time.

In our view, this is the strongest argument in the sample for having a second trusted admin on the Business Manager from day one. Our ad account access checklist and our note on who owns your ad account explain how to set that up without sharing logins.

Business Manager restrictions did not come back

22 reports involved a Business Manager or business portfolio. 7 were not restored and 15 had no outcome. Owners described being unable to remove a Page, a pixel or a catalog from a restricted Business Manager, which then blocked them from connecting those assets anywhere else. Two lost access after missing a verification request that arrived while they were not advertising.

Ads rejected in bulk: the group most likely to recover

10 reports were about ads rejected again and again while the account stayed open. 3 of them got their ads running again, the best rate of any level. An in-house marketer at a consumer brand spending over $20k a month saw every version of a launch ad blocked for "unlikely results", and wrote that each time "our appeal would come back "sorry, we didn't mean to block your ad."" A dating app add-on was banned and unbanned several times as it rewrote its copy. Our guide to Meta special ad categories covers the restricted categories that trip up legitimate advertisers.

The notices were vague, and the advertisers' guesses were spread thin. 32 (32.7%) had no idea. 20 (20.4%) were restricted on a brand new account, at or before their first ad. One new store owner wrote: "My Ad Account was very literally disabled no less than 60 seconds after trying to use my Ad Account for the first time." 7 more were dormant accounts, or accounts that had never advertised, restricted months or years after their last use.

9 (9.2%) were hacked first. Attackers ran their own ads on the victim's card, and in 4 of these the advertiser also lost the personal profile. A clothing store owner who had spent about $12k on ads in 18 months was still locked out after the hack: "now it has been more than 2 months, my account is disabled, the money spend by the hacker are not refunded".

Payment came up in 4 reports. A software startup founder noted that "The restriction email came immediately after a failed billing notification". The account was closed for good after two appeals.

14 advertisers (14.3%) could point to something in the ad, the product or the landing page, such as a gift card offer, a trademark, a dating app or a cookie app flagged as malware. They had the best odds of any cause: 4 of the 14 (28.6%) got access back. In our view, that is the most useful finding here. A restriction you can explain is one a reviewer can reverse.

The review path: request, ID, a person

47 advertisers (48%) said they requested a review. 9 of them (19.1%) were restored, 18 were refused and 20 were still waiting. 7 could not request one, or chose not to, and none of those 7 got back in. 9 of the 10 restorations in the sample came after a review request.

Reaching a person helped more than anything else, and it was rare. 15 advertisers (15.3%) reached someone at Meta by chat, phone or email, and 5 of them (33.3%) got access back. 28 said there was no way to reach a human. One advertiser spending over $500k a year wrote that "we were blocked by AI with no recourse for over 6 months before we finally found a human who was able to reverse it."

ID checks went the other way. 16 advertisers (16.3%) sent ID, a selfie or a video selfie. 1 was restored, 8 were refused and 7 were waiting. The exception was quick. A store owner restricted while connecting their store "Had to upload a picture of myself to verify my identity. It was resolved in 5-10 minutes." Three advertisers turned to someone who worked at Meta, and 1 of them was restored. Two disputed charges with their bank and one considered small claims court.

What the reports suggest you check before requesting review

These checks come only from what the 98 advertisers described. None of them guarantees a result. The official route is a review request from Account Quality, Meta's own page for restrictions on profiles, ad accounts, Pages and Business Managers.

  1. Which asset is restricted? 29 reports involved more than one level. Open Account Quality and note whether the notice sits on your profile, the ad account, the Business Manager or a Page before you write anything.
  2. Is your login still yours? 9 restrictions followed a hack. Secure the profile, remove unknown admins and payment methods, and note the dates and amounts of any ads you did not create.
  3. What did Meta actually say? 37 advertisers never said what the notice told them, and 15 got no reason at all. Copy the exact wording. If a policy is named, compare it with every live ad and the landing page.
  4. Is there something you can point to? Advertisers who could name a cause in the ad or page had the best odds of any cause, with 4 of 14 restored. Fix it before you request review, and say what you changed.
  5. Did a payment fail? 4 advertisers linked the restriction to a failed or changed card. Check the billing history for declined charges first.
  6. Is a verification request waiting? Two advertisers lost access after missing one. Check notifications and Business Settings even when no ads are running.
  7. Use your requests with care. One coaching business ran out after three review requests, and a startup was told it could not file another while one was pending. Send one complete request, with the fix described.
  8. Keep records, and ignore recovery offers. Screenshots helped one restaurant owner get charges refunded through the bank. We also saw three forum replies offering paid or back-channel recovery. In our view, treat any of those as a scam.

Our guide to a Meta ad account ban covers how to write the request itself, and our Meta ads pre-launch checklist and landing page checklist cover the checks worth running before the first ad.

What we would change, in our view

For advertisers, the sample points to prevention more than appeals. Set up the Business Manager with two admins and your own payment method before you need it, verify the business early, and keep the ad and the page saying the same thing. If you are a new store, expect scrutiny on your first ad and keep it plain. The owners in our Facebook ads owner study and the local businesses in our Facebook ads for local business guide show how much a small budget depends on one working account.

For Meta, the reports ask for two things: a specific reason, and a person to talk to. The advertisers who reached a person, or could name a cause, recovered more often than the sample as a whole.

This study uses the sources and rules of our owner studies on Google Ads, Facebook ads, Microsoft Ads, LinkedIn Ads, Performance Max, TikTok and Reddit Ads from 21 to 25 November 2026. We did not work around any block, CAPTCHA or login.

On the Shopify Community we ran 42 searches such as "facebook ad account disabled", "advertising access restricted", "business manager disabled", "circumventing systems" and "facebook ad account hacked". They returned 739 topics. We downloaded the 106 topics whose title names Facebook, Meta or Instagram with an advertising or restriction word, or names an ad account or Business Manager, and read every original post with a restriction word plus replies where another advertiser described their own restriction: 115 posts in 91 topics. On Hacker News we ran 37 comment searches and 8 post searches, read the 171 comments that paired a restriction phrase with a first-person word, and read 38 full threads.

A report counts when the writer's own Meta account or asset was disabled or restricted, or their ads were rejected over and over, for their own business or their employer. A personal profile counts only when the ban took their advertising, Business Manager or business Page with it, or they were setting up to advertise. We excluded agencies, consultants and ad-tech firms, people advertising ad services, second-hand stories, Facebook Shop, Commerce Manager and catalog rejections, data-category restrictions on datasets, personal bans with no business link, and anyone identifiably outside the US, UK, Canada and Australia. Three replies offering paid or back-channel recovery were read and excluded. People who posted more than once were matched by comparing authors in memory, and 12 repeat posts were merged into their author's main report. Each report was coded once, by one person.

Outcome rules. Restored means access or ads came back. Not restored means Meta called the decision final, a review was refused or impossible, or the writer gave up or was still locked out a year or more later. Unresolved means the writer was still waiting or did not say.

Limits. Everything is self-reported and unchecked, and we only hear the advertiser's side. People post while they are stuck, so 57 of 98 reports have no outcome, and someone who got back in rarely returns to say so. Shopify threads are help requests, mostly from new stores. Only 3 reports name a country. Meta changed its review tools many times between 2009 and 2026. No usernames are stored; the data file holds thread and post links only.

Questions about a disabled or restricted Facebook ad account

Why was my Facebook ad account disabled?

In our 98 reports, Meta most often cited its Advertising Policies (18), gave no reason (15) or cited Community Standards (13). Advertisers most often had no idea (32), had just opened the account (20), or could point to something in the ad or landing page (14). 9 had been hacked first.

Can a disabled Facebook ad account be restored?

Sometimes. Of the 41 reports with a known outcome, 10 were restored. Ads rejected in bulk recovered most often (3 of 10), while none of the 22 Business Manager restrictions and none of the 27 new or dormant accounts in the sample were lifted.

How long does a Meta ad account review take?

Only 20 advertisers said. One was restored 10 minutes after a selfie check and another after over 6 months. 12 of the 20 had waited a month or more, and most were still waiting when they posted.

Does requesting a review or sending ID help?

Requesting a review came before 9 of the 10 restorations, though 18 requests were refused. Reaching a person at Meta worked best, with 5 of 15 restored. Sending ID or a selfie led to 1 restoration out of 16.

Where do I request a review of a restricted ad account?

From Account Quality, Meta's own page for restrictions on profiles, ad accounts, Pages and Business Managers. Before you request one, check which asset is restricted, secure the login, read the exact reason, fix anything in the ad or landing page, and check billing for failed payments.
