Landing page statistics 2026: we opened the pages behind 420 US advertisers' Facebook ads on a phone

Ad platforms report the click. What happens after it is up to the landing page, so we opened 513 ad links from law firms, dentists, med spas, home service firms and real estate agents on an iPhone-sized screen and wrote down what a visitor gets.

Every Facebook ad with a website link hands the visitor to a landing page, and that page decides whether the click becomes a call, a form or nothing. We wanted to see what local businesses actually put there. So we took the 677 live US ads with a website link from our two Ad Library studies, covering law firms, dentists, med spas, home service firms and real estate agents, opened every link on an iPhone-sized screen on 7 November 2026, and recorded what a visitor gets.

The short version: of the 368 landing pages that loaded, 42.4% were the advertiser's home page, a third had no Meta pixel, and only 54.9% let a phone visitor tap to call without scrolling. Forms were usually below the first screen and asked five questions. Pages built just for the ad were lighter, had fewer ways to wander off and were much more likely to carry the pixel. The full dataset is free to download at the end.

What we checked on each landing page

We counted each advertiser once, on the link its ads used most. That gave 420 advertisers and 513 different links. 87.6% of the pages loaded and could be analysed. The rest were broken, blocked our visit, or stayed blank for us, and we cover those further down.

We expected most local businesses to use their website and few to build dedicated pages, and that held for law firms, home service firms and real estate agents. Dentists looked different, and one vendor explains most of it: 12 of the 50 dentist pages were near-identical implant funnels hosted on the same marketing company's domain, each for a different practice. Without them, 47.4% of dentist pages were home pages and 18.4% were separate landing pages.

The home page was the most common landing page

Home service firms sent people to their home page most often, at 60.4%. Med spas built the most separate landing pages, 40 of the 82 (28.8% of med spa pages), usually for a single offer such as a toxin price or a facial package. Dentists sent the most pages to someone else's site, 10% of them, mostly online booking tools and directory listings.

GoHighLevel built 47 of the 82 separate landing pages (57.3%). It showed up mostly in med spas (29 pages, 20.9% of the trade) and dentists (19 pages, 38%). WordPress was the most common platform overall, behind 33.7% of all pages. Six med spa pages were built with AI app builders, four of them on vercel.app addresses.

A home page has a job to do for everyone who finds the business, so it carries a menu, service lists and location pages. The median home page in our sample had about 30 internal links. Each one is a way out of the offer the ad just made. Home pages were also about three times as likely as separate landing pages to have no Meta pixel. Our view: a home page can work for a local firm whose ad simply says "call us", as long as the phone number is at the top. If the ad sells one offer, send the click to a page about that offer.

A third of the landing pages had no Meta pixel

122 of the 368 pages (33.2%) had no Meta pixel at all. These advertisers were paying Meta for the click, but Meta could not see whether anyone called, booked or filled in a form afterwards.

Home service firms were the most likely to have no pixel, at 41.5%. Dentists stood at 36%, but the implant vendor's pages all had one; without them, 47.4% of dentist pages had no pixel. Law firms were the best covered, and even there a quarter had none. GoHighLevel pages were the exception, with 85.2% carrying the pixel.

Another 32 pages had pixel code that never fired on a plain visit. Some wait for a click on a cookie banner; on others the code loads but never sends the page view. Either way, some visitors are never counted. Most pages without a Meta pixel did have a Google tag; 27 pages (7.3%) had neither, so neither Meta nor Google saw the visit.

One caveat. A business can send events to Meta from its server through the Conversions API, and we cannot see that from outside. In our experience small local advertisers rarely do it without the pixel. When Meta cannot see who acts, it optimises for clicks, and in our experience that brings cheap clicks from people who never call. Our guide to the Conversions API for lead businesses covers setting up both.

Can a phone visitor call from the first screen?

73.1% of pages had a tap-to-call link somewhere, but only 54.9% showed one on the first screen without scrolling. The first-screen share varied between trades more than almost anything else we measured: 81.6% for law firms against 35.3% for med spas.

Law firms treat the phone as the main conversion. 81.6% had a call link on the first screen, 46.1% used call tracking software that swaps in a tracked number, and 43.4% ran a chat widget, most often Ngage (13 pages) or Intaker (9). Med spas were at the other end, with a call link on the first screen of 35.3% of pages. Many med spas push online booking instead, and 13.7% of their pages loaded a booking widget, which is fine if the booking flow is quick on a phone.

The surprise for us was home services. Firms whose work starts with a phone call had a call link on the first screen of 71.7% of pages, yet only 28.3% tracked calls. Without tracking, a firm has no way to tell which calls came from the Facebook ad.

Forms sit low and ask five questions

149 pages (40.5%) showed a lead form with visible questions when they loaded. Only 30.9% of those put the form on the first screen; the rest made the visitor scroll to find it.

The median form asked 5 questions, and 45.6% asked 6 or more. Only 18.1% asked 3 or fewer. 78.5% of forms asked for an email address, 65.8% for a phone number and 51.7% had a message box. For a business that wants to call the lead back, asking for an email more often than a phone number is the wrong way round.

These counts leave out quiz-style pages that start with buttons and booking widgets loaded from other sites, so the true share of pages that collect details is higher. Our rule for a first enquiry on a phone is name, phone and one question that sorts serious leads from the rest. Everything else can wait for the call. Our post on form design goes through the questions we cut first.

How heavy the pages were

The median page transferred 2.5 MB on our phone-sized visit. HTTP Archive's 2025 Web Almanac puts the median mobile home page at 2.56 MB, so ad landing pages were about as heavy as the web in general. A quarter were over 4.1 MB, 16.8% were over 5 MB and 6.3% were over 10 MB, usually because of background video or full-size photos.

The median page made 93 requests and loaded 1.09 MB of JavaScript, and contacted a median of 11 outside domains. HTTP Archive's median mobile home page makes 75 requests and loads 632 KB of JavaScript. Real estate pages were the heaviest at a 3.8 MB median, with listing photos and maps; law firm pages were the lightest at 2 MB. We did not time the loads, because our visits came from India and the distance would distort them. A heavy page is slower on a weak phone connection, and a slow page loses some of the people the ad just paid for.

Does the page keep the ad's promise?

Mostly, yes. 46 of the advertisers whose page loaded had ads promising something free, such as a consultation, an estimate or a case review. 38 of them (82.6%) mentioned the free offer in the page's opening text. Among law firms it was 21 of 25.

20 pages (5.4%) covered the middle of the screen with an offer, sign-up or announcement pop-up within seconds of loading, and 18 of them were med spas (12.9% of med spa pages). A pop-up that asks for an email before the visitor has seen the offer the ad promised is a strange way to greet someone who already clicked. Reviews were easier to find: 53.3% of pages mentioned reviews or ratings, rising to 71.7% for home service firms.

Broken and blocked links

11 of the 420 advertisers' links (2.6%) were broken. They led to domains that no longer resolved, error pages, certificates browsers reject, or a site set to private. If those ads are still running, they are paying for clicks that go nowhere.

Another 30 (7.1%) blocked our visit with a security check or a rule that turns away visitors from outside the US. A US visitor on a phone may well get through, so we do not count these as broken. Still, a check that stops a real visitor wastes the click just the same, so it is worth opening your own ad on your own phone.

What we would change after opening 513 ad links

  • Put a tap-to-call button on the first screen. Just over half the pages already do. Make it stay on screen while the visitor scrolls.
  • Check the pixel on the page your ads actually use. A third of pages had none. Open the page with Meta's test events tool and confirm it fires.
  • Match the page to the offer. If the ad sells one thing, send the click to a page about that thing, with the offer in the headline and few links out.
  • Shorten the form and move it up. Ask for a phone number, put the form on the first screen or one tap away, and save the rest for the call.
  • Track calls. If calls are your main lead, you need to know which came from the ad.
  • Open your own ad once a month. Broken links, slow pages and pop-ups covering the offer are easy to miss from a desktop.

The ads come from our two Meta Ad Library studies of 3 November 2026: one of law firms, dentists, real estate and home services, and one of med spas. We took every included ad whose destination was a website: 677 ads with 513 different links from 420 advertisers. Each advertiser counts once, on the link its ads used most, so a chain that ran dozens of links counts the same as a single practice.

On 7 November 2026 we opened each link once in Google Chrome running without a window on our own computer, set to an iPhone screen of 390 by 844 pixels with an iPhone Safari browser identity, a browser profile used only for this crawl, the cache switched off and no cookie banners accepted. After the page loaded we waited for its network activity to stop, then moved the mouse, scrolled and pressed a key, because many sites hold scripts back until the visitor moves. We waited again, scrolled back to the top and read the page. We never filled in or submitted a form, and we did not try to get past security checks.

The first screen means anything whose top edge sat within the first 844 pixels. A lead form is a form with at least two visible questions, or a single phone-number box; search boxes, logins and single-box email sign-ups do not count, and radio buttons or tick boxes that share a name count as one question. Quiz pages that start with buttons, and booking or form widgets loaded from another site, are counted separately. Tap-to-call means a visible tel: link. The Meta pixel counts as installed if its code was in the page, its fbq function existed or the browser called facebook.com/tr, and as firing only if that call happened with no click on any cookie banner. Page weight is the data transferred until the network went quiet. Platforms were identified from the files each page loaded. We read every element that covered the middle of the screen and sorted it by hand.

We visited from India, so we do not report load times, and some of the 30 blocked visits were location rules a US visitor would not meet. Those 30, the 11 broken links and 11 links that did not load or showed a blank screen for us are left out of the page figures, which cover 368 pages. The ads were collected four days before the visits, so a few may have ended. Tracking that runs only on a server (the Conversions API without the pixel) cannot be seen from outside, so the pixel figures describe the browser side. The advertisers are the ones Meta returned for our searches, which cover part of each trade. The CSV has one row per advertiser and no names or links.

Questions about ad landing pages

Should Facebook ads go to a home page or a landing page?

Of the 368 pages in our study that loaded, 42.4% were the advertiser's home page and 22.3% a separate landing page. The separate pages were lighter (2.1 MB median against 2.7 MB), had a median of 2 internal links against about 30, and were missing the Meta pixel far less often (14.6% against 44.2%). If the ad sells one offer, we send it to a page about that offer.

How many fields should a landing page form have?

The median form in our study asked 5 questions, and 45.6% asked 6 or more. For a first enquiry from a phone we keep it to name, phone and one qualifying question, and ask the rest on the call.

How heavy is a typical ad landing page?

The median page in our study transferred 2.5 MB on a phone, close to the 2.56 MB median for mobile home pages that HTTP Archive reported for 2025. 16.8% of pages were over 5 MB and 6.3% were over 10 MB.

Do I need the Meta pixel on my landing page?

If you want Meta to find people who call, book or fill in the form, it has to see those actions through the pixel, the Conversions API or both. 33.2% of the pages we checked had no pixel at all, and another 32 had pixel code that did not fire on a plain visit.

How did you check the landing pages?

We opened each ad link once on 7 November 2026 in Chrome set to an iPhone screen, waited for the page to settle, scrolled once and read the page. We never submitted a form. Pages that blocked the visit or failed to load are left out, leaving 368 pages from 420 advertisers.
