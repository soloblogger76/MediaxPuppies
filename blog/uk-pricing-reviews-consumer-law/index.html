Drip pricing and fake reviews: the UK rules that reach your landing page

A conversion tactic that works by surprising people at checkout is now a legal exposure as well as a reason they do not come back.

Media Puppies · 9 October 2026 · Updated 15 October 2026

Drip pricing and fake reviews used to be filed under "aggressive but normal" in UK ecommerce have moved into enforceable consumer protection territory: revealing mandatory charges late, and reviews that are not what they appear to be.

Both live on the landing page and the checkout, which is to say: on the pages your ad budget is pointing at.

Drip pricing and what counts as hidden

Drip pricing is showing a headline price and adding unavoidable charges as the customer progresses. A ticket at twenty-four pounds that becomes thirty-one at checkout because of a mandatory booking fee is the standard example.

The distinction that matters is unavoidable. Genuinely optional extras — insurance, faster delivery, gift wrap — are a different thing. A fee every customer must pay belongs in the price shown up front.

From a purely commercial angle this was always a bad trade. Hidden fees produce basket abandonment, chargebacks and customers who do not return. You pay to acquire somebody and then spend that goodwill annoying them at the last step.

Reviews

Fake reviews, reviews written by people with an undisclosed connection to the business, and incentivised reviews presented as spontaneous are all problems. So is selectively publishing only the positive ones while implying the set is complete.

Asking customers for reviews is fine and good. Offering a discount for one is where it gets complicated, and presenting that as an ordinary unprompted review is where it stops being complicated and starts being a problem.

If a reasonable customer would feel differently about the review after learning how it was obtained, it needs a disclosure.

Why this is a paid media issue

Three reasons.

The landing page is part of the advertisement. An ad promising a price that the page then adds to is misleading regardless of where the misleading bit happens.

Review widgets feed your ad extensions and rich results. Ratings pulled into Google results or shopping listings are claims you are making in the advert.

Your conversion rate is lying to you. A page with hidden fees shows a healthy add-to-cart rate and a poor completion rate, and teams spend months optimising the wrong step. We have audited accounts where the single biggest available improvement was showing the real price earlier.

What to check on your own site

  1. Is every unavoidable charge in the price shown on the first page a customer sees?
  2. Does the ad price match the page price?
  3. Where do reviews come from, and was anything given in exchange?
  4. Does the widget show all reviews, or a filtered set presented as all?
  5. Does a star rating in your ad match what the page actually shows?

The commercial case

Set the compliance argument aside for a moment. Transparent pricing converts worse at the top of the funnel and better at the bottom, and the bottom is the part you get paid for. Customers who were not surprised at checkout return more often, refund less, and complain less.

If your acquisition economics only work because some customers do not notice a fee until the end, that is not a conversion strategy. It is a churn strategy with a delay built in.

Worth running your real numbers through the contribution margin calculator with your actual refund rate in it — the picture usually looks different once returns and chargebacks are counted.

Questions people ask

What is drip pricing?

Showing a headline price and revealing mandatory charges later in the journey, so the amount payable only becomes clear at checkout. Where a fee is unavoidable, UK rules expect it in the price the customer first sees.

Are booking or service fees still allowed?

Yes, when they are genuinely optional or genuinely variable. What is not allowed is presenting an unavoidable charge as an extra discovered at the end, which is the pattern enforcement focuses on.

What counts as a fake review?

Reviews written by or for the business, incentivised reviews presented as independent, and suppressing negative ones so the overall picture misleads. Hosting reviews also brings an expectation that you take reasonable steps to check them.

Why is this a paid media problem?

Because the landing page is part of the advertising. A price in the ad that does not survive to checkout is both a consumer law exposure and a conversion problem, and platforms enforce their own version of the same rule.
