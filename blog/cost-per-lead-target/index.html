How to set your own cost per lead target

Every benchmark you can find was measured on somebody else’s business. The only cost per lead that matters is the one your own numbers allow.

Media Puppies · 11 October 2026

A cost per lead target is the one number every paid lead generation account needs, and the usual way of setting it — an industry average from a report — is almost useless. Averages blend businesses with different close rates, different deal sizes and different margins. Two companies in the same industry can have acceptable costs per lead that differ by a factor of ten.

The number you need is not a benchmark. It is a calculation, and it takes five minutes.

Work backwards from a customer

Start at the end and move left.

  1. What is a customer worth to you in gross profit? Not revenue. Revenue minus cost of goods or cost of delivery. If customers buy repeatedly, use a conservative view of lifetime value rather than an optimistic one — the LTV to CAC ratio covers how conservative to be.
  2. What share of that are you willing to spend to acquire them? This is a business decision, not a marketing one. A business funding growth from cash flow spends less of it than one funding growth from capital. Whatever you choose, that number is your maximum cost per acquisition.
  3. What share of leads become customers? Your actual close rate from this channel, not your overall one. Referral leads close far better than cold paid leads and mixing them will flatter the answer.
  4. Divide. Maximum cost per acquisition multiplied by close rate gives your maximum cost per lead.

That last step is the whole thing. If you can afford a certain amount to win a customer, and one lead in ten becomes a customer, you can afford a tenth of that per lead. The lead funnel calculator runs the chain for you, including the stages in between.

The stage most people forget

Between a form fill and a customer there is usually a step that loses more than anything else: whether the lead is contactable at all. A meaningful share of paid leads never answer, and that share varies enormously by channel and by how fast you call. If you only measure cost per form fill, you are measuring the cheapest point in the funnel rather than a useful one.

Measure cost per contacted lead and cost per qualified lead as well. The ranking of your channels frequently changes between those three numbers, and the channel with the cheapest form fills is often not the one producing customers. Why paid leads do not answer is a solvable problem, and solving it moves your allowable cost per lead more than any bidding change.

A cheaper lead that nobody can reach is not cheaper. It is a smaller number in a column that does not pay salaries.

What to do with your cost per lead target

Three things change immediately.

You can judge a channel in a week. Instead of asking whether a cost per lead feels high, you compare it with a threshold you derived yourself. A high cost per lead stops being an anxiety and becomes a diagnosis.

You can scale with confidence. If leads are coming in comfortably under the ceiling, you have room to bid up, and bidding up buys volume. Most accounts are more constrained by caution than by budget.

You can brief an agency properly. Handing an agency your allowable cost per lead and the close rate behind it is the single most useful thing a client can do. It turns a vague objective into a constraint everyone can optimise against, and it is the first thing we ask for in a free audit.

Review your target cost per lead, do not set it once

Close rates move when the sales team changes, when the offer changes, and when the mix of channels changes. Margins move with costs. Recalculate quarterly, and recalculate immediately after any pricing change — a price rise raises your allowable cost per lead, which usually means you can buy volume your competitors cannot.

If your close rate is the weak link rather than your media, that is a different project. Feeding sales outcomes back into the ad platforms is how the two sides stop arguing about lead quality, and it is a large part of how we run lead generation accounts.

Questions people ask before hiring

What is a good cost per lead target?

There is no universal figure. A good cost per lead is any number below what your gross profit per customer and your close rate allow, which is a calculation specific to your business rather than an industry average.

What should my cost per lead be?

Whatever your own numbers allow. Take the gross profit a customer is worth, decide what share of it you are willing to spend to acquire them, and multiply that by your close rate from the channel in question. The result is your ceiling per lead, and it is specific to you.

Should I use lifetime value or first-order profit?

Use first-order profit if cash is tight, and a conservative lifetime value if you can fund the gap. Spending against optimistic lifetime value is the most common way a profitable-looking account runs a business out of money.

Why do industry benchmarks vary so much?

Because they average businesses with different deal sizes, close rates and margins. Two companies in the same category can have ceilings that differ tenfold, which makes the average true of neither.

What should I measure instead of cost per form fill?

Cost per contacted lead and cost per qualified lead. Channel rankings often change between those measures, and the channel with the cheapest form fills is frequently not the one producing customers.
