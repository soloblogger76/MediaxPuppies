Google Ads management cost: pricing models, fees and what you should pay for

Management fees are quoted in three incompatible ways, which is what makes comparing them so difficult. Here is how to translate between them.

Media Puppies · 11 October 2026 · Updated 27 October 2026

Google Ads management cost is the fee an agency or freelancer charges to run your Google Ads account. It is separate from your ad spend, which you pay to Google directly. Agencies price management in three main ways: a flat monthly fee, a percentage of ad spend, or a performance fee tied to leads or sales, sometimes with a setup fee on top.

Which one is fair depends less on the headline number and more on two questions: what work does your account actually need, and what does each pricing model reward the agency for doing? This guide explains each pricing model, what pushes Google Ads management fees up, what should be included, how costs differ between agencies, freelancers and in-house hires, and how to judge a quote before you sign it.

Google Ads management cost versus ad spend

Every Google Ads arrangement has two separate costs.

  • Ad spend is what Google charges for clicks, calls and impressions. It should be billed to your card or your invoice account, inside an ad account you own.
  • Management cost is what you pay the agency for the work: structure, keywords, bidding, ads, tracking, testing and reporting.

Keep them apart. If an agency asks to bill your ad spend through its own account, you lose visibility of what Google actually charged, and you may lose the account history if you leave. Who owns your ad account explains why this matters more than the fee itself.

The three Google Ads management pricing models

Flat monthly fee. A fixed fee set by the work the account needs. It is predictable for you, and it is the only model where advice to reduce spend costs the agency nothing. The fee changes when the scope changes: a new market, a new campaign type, a bigger catalogue.

Percentage of ad spend. Commonly a tenth to a fifth of what you spend, often with a minimum. It is easy to quote and easy to understand, and structurally awkward: the agency is paid more for spending more, which makes "pause this campaign" an expensive sentence to say. The incentive each fee model creates outlives everybody's good intentions.

Performance or per-lead pricing. Paid on outcomes. It sounds like the fairest model and is usually worse than it looks, because it pushes the agency toward whatever converts quickest: branded searches, remarketing to people who were already coming back, and demand you already had. Pay-per-lead arrangements deserve a careful read before signing.

Some agencies combine models, such as a base fee plus a smaller percentage above a spend threshold. Hybrids can work, but they are harder to compare. Convert every quote into the same shape: what you would pay per month at your current spend, and at double it.

How percentage-of-spend fees behave as you grow

The difference between pricing models shows up when spend changes. Say a business spends $10,000 a month on Google Ads and grows to $30,000 over a year because the account is working.

Under a percentage-of-spend model, the management fee triples, even if the work did not. The keywords, campaign structure and reporting may be much the same; the account is simply bigger. Under a flat fee, the fee stays the same unless the scope genuinely grows, for example by adding Shopping campaigns or a new country.

The reverse matters too. If the right move is to cut spend, perhaps because a campaign stopped working or the season ended, a percentage model cuts the agency's income. A flat fee does not, so the advice you get is not shaped by the fee.

Setup fees: when they are fair

A setup fee is fair when it pays for work the monthly fee clearly does not cover, and when that work is listed. Rebuilding broken conversion tracking, restructuring an account with years of clutter, or building a Shopping feed from scratch are real one-off jobs. A setup fee that simply describes the first month of normal management is the first month being charged twice.

Ask for the setup work to be itemised, with a date by which each item will be finished. If the agency cannot list what the setup fee buys, it is not buying anything specific.

What pushes Google Ads management fees up

  • Campaign types. Search alone is a smaller job than Search plus Shopping plus Performance Max plus YouTube.
  • Catalogue size. A feed with twenty products and one with eight thousand are different disciplines. Feed work is often the biggest lever in ecommerce and often left out of a low quote.
  • Markets and languages. Each is effectively a separate account, with its own structure, ads and reporting.
  • Measurement state. An account that needs conversion tracking rebuilt, or server-side tracking set up, costs more in month one than one that is already clean.
  • Creative scope. Ad copy and direction are normally included. Shoots, video and heavy design usually are not, and this is the most common gap between quote and invoice.
  • Regulated categories. Finance, health and anything that needs compliance review on every ad takes more hours and more care.

Cheap Google Ads management: what you usually lose

A low quote is not always a bad quote, but it is always paid for somewhere. The usual trade-offs:

  • Fewer hours. The search terms report gets read monthly instead of weekly, and wasted spend runs longer.
  • A more junior buyer. The senior person on the sales call hands the account to someone learning on it.
  • More accounts per person. A buyer with forty accounts cannot think about yours often.
  • Automation left on defaults. Performance Max and broad match run without brand exclusions or negative keywords, and report a return you already had.

Each of these costs more in wasted ad spend than it saves in fees, usually within a quarter. The difference is just harder to see, because it shows up in the Google invoice rather than the agency one.

A worked comparison of three quotes

Say a UK home improvement company spends 15,000 pounds a month and receives three quotes. These are hypothetical, to show how to compare structures rather than headline numbers.

  • Quote A is a percentage of spend with a monthly minimum. It covers Search only, with monthly reporting and a 12-month contract.
  • Quote B is a flat monthly fee. It covers Search and Performance Max, conversion tracking fixes in month one, weekly notes and monthly reports, on a rolling monthly contract.
  • Quote C is a low flat fee plus a payment per lead. It covers Search, with leads counted from form fills only.

Converted to the same shape, Quote A costs more each month as the account grows and locks the company in for a year. Quote C looks cheapest, but counts every form fill as a lead, including spam and job seekers, and rewards the agency for volume rather than customers. Quote B costs more than C at today's spend, includes the tracking work the account needs, and can be cancelled if results disappoint. Most businesses in that position should take B, or ask A to match its scope and terms.

Agency, freelancer or in-house: how the cost compares

  • Agency. A team, cover for holidays, and experience across many accounts. You pay for structure and continuity.
  • Freelancer. Often lower cost and very flexible, with more risk when the person is ill, busy or moves on.
  • In-house specialist. The deepest knowledge of your business, plus salary, benefits, tools and recruitment time. It makes sense once Google Ads is big enough to keep a senior person busy all year.

Google Ads management cost in the US, UK and Australia

The pricing models are the same in all three markets. The practical differences are tax and currency. UK businesses buying management from an overseas agency usually account for VAT themselves under the reverse charge, and Australian businesses may have a similar obligation for GST on imported services, so ask your accountant how invoices from abroad are treated. US businesses generally pay no sales tax on agency services, though rules vary by state. Whatever the market, the contract should state the fee in your currency so exchange rates do not move it.

Contract terms that change the real cost

The fee is only part of the price. Terms in the contract can cost more than the fee itself.

  • Minimum term. A 6 or 12-month lock-in means you pay for poor results until it ends. Rolling monthly terms keep the agency honest.
  • Notice period. Thirty days is normal. Ninety days is a lock-in by another name.
  • Account ownership. The ad account, Google Analytics and Merchant Center should be in your name, with the agency added as a user.
  • Scope changes. Agree in advance how a new market or campaign type changes the fee, so it does not become a surprise invoice.
  • Exit handover. The contract should say you keep everything built during the engagement: campaigns, audiences, conversion actions and reports.

Red flags in a Google Ads management quote

  • Setup fees that duplicate the first month's normal work.
  • Charges per report or per call.
  • Fees to access your own data or ad accounts.
  • Exit fees, or long lock-in periods with no performance review.
  • Ad spend billed through the agency rather than directly by Google.

If any of these appear, ask what they buy. The answer is usually revenue smoothing rather than work.

How to judge the number you have been given

Three questions do most of the work. What exactly is in scope, in writing? Who runs the account day to day, and how many other accounts do they carry? What happens if you want to stop next month? An agency that answers all three without hesitating is probably charging what the work costs. One that deflects on any of them is pricing something other than the work.

Compare the fee with your ad spend as well as with other quotes. If management costs about as much as the media budget, most of your money is buying attention rather than customers, and the budget may be too small for any agency arrangement to pay back. Questions to ask before you sign

  1. What exactly is included each month, and what would cost extra?
  2. Who will work on our account, and how many other accounts do they manage?
  3. Will the ad account and billing stay in our name?
  4. How often will you read the search terms report and change the account?
  5. What will you fix in the first 30 days?
  6. How do you report, and can we see a sample?
  7. What is the notice period, and what do we keep if we leave?

Clear answers to these tell you more than any comparison of fees. Our guide to choosing a Google Ads agency covers the follow-up questions.

When to review your Google Ads management fee

Review the fee when the scope changes, not on a calendar alone. Adding a country, launching Shopping, or doubling the number of campaigns is real extra work. So is the reverse: if an account has been simplified, the fee should come down. A yearly check against the original scope is a good habit, and a fair agency will raise scope changes before you do.

Questions people ask before hiring

Do I pay Google separately from the management fee?

Yes. Ad spend should be billed by Google directly to you, inside an ad account you own. The management fee is a separate invoice from the agency.

Why do some agencies charge a percentage of ad spend?

It is simple to quote and scales automatically with account size. The drawback is that the agency earns more when you spend more, whether or not results improve.

Is there a minimum budget for Google Ads management?

Below a certain spend, an account cannot gather enough conversions to optimise, so management fees take too large a share. A good agency will tell you on the first call whether your budget is big enough to benefit.

Is a percentage of ad spend a fair way to charge for Google Ads?

It is common and it is simple, but it ties the agency’s income to your budget rather than your results. The problem shows up on the day the right recommendation is to spend less, which is a recommendation that model makes expensive to give.

What is a reasonable Google Ads management cost compared with ad spend?

There is no universal ratio, but the fee should be a modest fraction of media rather than a comparable amount. If management costs about as much as the ads, the budget is too small for an agency to add value at that fee.

How much does Google Ads management cost per month?

There is no single figure, because PPC management cost tracks the work rather than the account. Campaign types, catalogue size, number of markets and the state of your tracking each move it. A cheap quote usually means fewer hours or a more junior buyer, and both show up within a quarter.

Should I pay a setup fee?

Only if it buys work that the retainer clearly does not, and only if that work is itemised. A setup fee that simply describes the first month of normal account work is the first month being charged twice.

What is included in Google Ads management, normally?

Account structure, keyword and negative keyword work, bidding and budget management, ad copy, feed and shopping management where relevant, conversion tracking oversight and regular reporting. Creative production and landing page builds usually sit outside it.
