Does Google penalise AI content? What actually happens

The short answer is no — and the long answer is why plenty of AI content still tanks, which has nothing to do with how it was written.

Media Puppies · 19 August 2026 · Updated 15 October 2026

Does Google penalise AI content? The question comes up on almost every SEO call now, usually phrased nervously. The short answer: Google does not penalise content for being AI-written. It penalises content for being unhelpful. Those two things overlap heavily in practice, which is why the myth persists.

Does Google penalise AI content: what Google has actually said

Google's stated position is about quality and usefulness, not production method. Content written to help a reader is fine regardless of how it was produced. Content mass-generated primarily to manipulate rankings is not — and that has been true since long before AI could write.

The important nuance: scaled content abuse is a named violation. The trigger is not the tool. It is producing large volumes of low-value pages whose main purpose is to rank.

So why do so many AI-content sites collapse?

Because of what people do with the tool, not the tool. The failure pattern is remarkably consistent:

  • Volume as the strategy. Two hundred pages in a month on a site with no authority. This is a visible pattern, and it is the one thing that most reliably attracts a demotion.
  • No first-hand anything. No data, no screenshots, no numbers from real accounts, no opinion that could be wrong. Just a competent summary of what already ranks.
  • Answers with nothing in them. Eight hundred words that, compressed, say it depends on your business.
  • Nobody read it before publishing. Wrong facts, hallucinated statistics, prices from another country.

None of this needs AI. Content farms have done all four with human writers for twenty years, and got demoted for it then too. AI just made it cheap enough for everyone.

Google is not asking who typed it. It is asking whether anyone needed it.

The rules we actually follow

We use AI in our writing process every day. These are the constraints that keep it working:

  1. Every article contains something only we could write. A real number from a real account, a mistake we made, a pattern from client work. If the piece would be identical coming from any agency, we do not publish it.
  2. A human names the angle before generation starts. Not the topic — the angle. What do we think, and why might a reader disagree?
  3. Every factual claim gets checked. Especially numbers. Especially anything about platform policy, which changes and which models remember wrongly.
  4. Publishing pace stays human. A few genuinely considered pieces beat a content calendar nobody reads.
  5. The final edit is a rewrite, not a proofread. If it still reads with that even AI rhythm, it gets another pass.

What about disclosure?

Google does not require you to label AI assistance, and labelling does not improve rankings. Decide it on your own principles rather than on SEO grounds. What matters far more is accuracy and whether a named, accountable human stands behind it.

The practical test

Before publishing anything, ask: if a knowledgeable customer read this, would they learn something, or would they recognise it as filler? If it is filler, it will not rank for long no matter who wrote it — and it will quietly cost you credibility with the people who did read it.

Questions people ask

Does Google penalise AI-written content?

Not for being AI-written. Google’s stated position is that it rewards helpful, original content however it was produced, and acts against content made primarily to manipulate rankings. The method is not the problem; scaled, unchecked output is.

Why do AI-content sites collapse then?

Because publishing at volume without editing produces pages that say nothing new. They are not penalised for provenance — they simply have nothing a reader could not get elsewhere, and that is what the helpful content systems demote.

Do I need to disclose that AI was used?

Google does not require it. Disclose if your audience would feel misled otherwise, which depends more on your category than on search. What matters to rankings is whether a person checked the claims before publishing.

What is the practical test?

Would a knowledgeable reader learn something they could not get from the first three results? If not, the article should not be published, regardless of who or what wrote it.
