Do LinkedIn Ads work for small business and B2B? We coded 37 owners' own reports

We searched public forums for founders and in-house marketers describing LinkedIn Ads they ran themselves and found 37 reports. More said it failed than said it worked, and the failures sound alike: clicks arrived, leads did not.

Do LinkedIn Ads work for small business? In the 37 first-hand reports we could find in public forums, 13 people (35.1%) said their LinkedIn Ads worked, 4 had mixed results, 15 said they did not work and 5 could not tell. Most of the failures describe the same thing: clicks arrived and leads did not.

This is the fourth study in a series. Our Google Ads owner study coded 89 reports, our Facebook ads owner study coded 100 and our Microsoft Ads owner study found 23. We used the same sources and rules here and aimed for at least 40 reports. We found 37, three short. We say that up front because it shapes everything below: each split is a small group of people. We run LinkedIn accounts ourselves and label our opinions. None of our own client or account data is in here.

Where the 37 reports come from

On 24 November 2026 we searched Hacker News and the Shopify Community, two public forums that load without a login. Reddit blocked plain requests three days earlier, so we did not try it, and we did not work around any block.

On Hacker News we ran 60 searches such as "linkedin ads", "sponsored inmail" and "linkedin lead gen forms", which returned 9,554 comments. Two text filters cut those to 198 that mention LinkedIn ads near a first-person phrase, and we read every one. Most were about job hunting, recruiter messages or ads people saw in their feed. We also read 15 threads about LinkedIn ads in full. The Shopify Community gave us 1 report. Its LinkedIn threads are almost all about adding a LinkedIn icon to a store footer, which tells you something about how few online stores run LinkedIn Ads at all.

LinkedIn is a B2B channel, so we widened one rule from the earlier studies. An in-house marketer who ran the company's own LinkedIn campaigns counts here, as long as they ran the ads themselves. Agencies describing client accounts still do not. Hiring ads do not count either.

We expected LinkedIn to look better than its reputation among small businesses, because the people who write about it on Hacker News mostly sell software to other businesses, which is the use LinkedIn is built for. It did not.

Leave out the 5 who could not tell and 13 of the remaining 32 (40.6%) said LinkedIn Ads worked. That is a weaker result than the Microsoft Ads owners gave in our study last week, where more than half of those with a verdict said yes.

The surprise is the software group. 18 of the 37 reports came from people selling SaaS or other software, mostly to businesses. Only 6 of those 18 said it worked, and 9 said it did not. If any small business should do well on LinkedIn, it is a B2B software company, and in this sample they did no better than everyone else.

Time did not change the picture much either. Before 2020, 5 of the 14 people with a clear verdict said it worked. From 2020 on, 8 of 18 did. In our view, that small rise is too thin to call a trend.

Clicks, then nothing. 9 of the 15 owners who said no described paid clicks that produced no sign-ups, demos or form fills. A B2B SaaS founder who built his own sign-up tracking found that "the actual conversions were a big fat zero." Another B2B SaaS founder stopped a test because visitors stayed for seconds: "At 2 seconds per $9.78 CPC I'll stay on Adwords." One small team saw about 30 clicks reported over two weeks and not one request in its server logs.

Too little delivery. Two early advertisers could barely get their ads seen. One B2B seller bid $4 a click for a week and got no clicks from 15,000 impressions. Another had such a low click-through rate that LinkedIn appeared to stop showing the ads.

Targeting is what the winners mentioned. The people who said it worked talked about leads and reach: a team filling live events for doctors, a founder who wanted meetings with a very specific set of professionals, an in-house marketer whose single lead from one campaign was worth more than the budget. In our view, this is the core of it. LinkedIn works when a narrow audience is worth a lot per person, and fails when it is asked to produce cheap volume.

5 owners named cost as a problem, and none of those 5 said LinkedIn worked outright: 3 were mixed and 2 said it failed. The clearest line came from an owner who had spent more than $10,000 of his own money on LinkedIn: "Think $50-200 per qualified lead, so it only makes sense for very high ticket offers." He said he had never made it work for offers under about $500 of gross margin per sale.

A marketer who ran a $10,000 test across four platforms in three weeks for a friend's startup came to a similar view from the other side. In his test, LinkedIn was the only platform that still delivered real people: "LinkedIn does still work, but it has gotten extremely expensive." How LinkedIn compared with Google and Meta

11 of the 37 compared LinkedIn with another platform they had also run, usually Google Ads or Meta. 6 said LinkedIn did worse, 3 said it did better and 2 said about the same. One B2B app founder put LinkedIn first for one job only, booking early customer interviews: "The most success we had was with LinkedIn Lead Gen forms."

That founder was also the only one of the 37 to name an ad format at all. Nobody mentioned document ads, conversation ads or message ads. We cannot tell you from this sample which format works best, and we would distrust anyone who claimed forum data could.

Two more patterns stand out. All 4 owners who said how long they ran ads stopped inside a month, and none of them said it worked. And 4 reports complained about the ad platform itself, from slow ad approvals to a campaign manager where finding last week's ads was a chore. In our view, short tests on LinkedIn mostly measure the learning curve.

What we would change (our view)

In our view, LinkedIn Ads suit a small business that sells something expensive to a narrow, nameable group of professionals, and that can wait for a sales cycle. They rarely suit a low-priced product, a self-serve sign-up, or a business that needs cheap volume. 9 of the 15 failures in this sample were software sellers, mostly chasing self-serve sign-ups. These are the checks we would run first:

  1. Can one customer pay for the lead cost? Owners here quoted $25 to $200 a lead. If a customer is worth less than a few hundred dollars of margin, start with Google search instead. Our B2B PPC guide covers how the two work together.
  2. Can you name the audience? Write down the job titles, company sizes and industries. If the list is vague, LinkedIn's main strength goes unused.
  3. Is the offer a meeting, a guide or a form? The successes here asked for a conversation or a lead form. A free trial with a cold audience was where most failures sat.
  4. Will you count leads beyond the platform's report? Check form fills and sales against your own CRM or server logs. Two owners here found the numbers did not match, and our guide to offline conversion import for SaaS covers how to send real outcomes back.
  5. Will you give it more than a month? Every short test in this sample was abandoned. Set a budget for at least a full month and judge it on cost per qualified lead.

If you want a second pair of eyes, our LinkedIn ads agency page explains how we run these campaigns, and our free ad account audit starts with the lead tracking checks above.

For agencies serving small businesses, these 37 people tell a plain story. LinkedIn can find the right person, it charges for the privilege, and it only pays off when that person is worth a lot.

This study copies the method of our Google Ads, Facebook ads and Microsoft Ads owner studies from 21 to 23 November 2026. Reddit blocked plain requests on 21 November, so we did not try it, and we did not work around any block, CAPTCHA or login. We used two sources that are readable without a login: Hacker News, through its public search API, and the Shopify Community, through its public JSON.

On Hacker News we ran 60 comment searches in two rounds, such as "linkedin ads", "linkedin advertising", "sponsored inmail", "linkedin lead gen forms", "linkedin cpc" and "linkedin ads b2b", and kept the 9,554 comments they returned. Two text filters cut those to 198 that mention LinkedIn ads near a first-person phrase, and we read all 198. We also searched 444 Hacker News posts and read 15 threads about LinkedIn ads in full. On the Shopify Community we ran 12 searches, found 120 topics and read the 38 that named LinkedIn or ad platforms. Almost all were about adding a LinkedIn icon to a store footer. Some search pages returned a rate-limit error and were not retried more than once.

A report counts when the writer ran LinkedIn Ads themselves, for their own business or as the in-house marketer, and says something about the result. We excluded agencies and freelancers describing client accounts, job ads and hiring posts, job seekers, people describing ads they saw, LinkedIn InMail used for support, organic posting and outreach, plans that had not run yet, and anyone identifiably outside the US, UK, Canada and Australia (one consulting business aimed at Spain). Where one person posted several times, we checked the author in memory and counted the person once: 5 repeat posts were merged. Each report was coded once, by one person.

Limits. 37 reports is a small sample, and every split in this article is a handful of people. Everything is self-reported and unchecked. Hacker News leans toward software founders, and 18 of the 37 sell software. 16 reports predate 2020. Only 9 gave a cost or spend figure and only 4 said how long they ran the ads. No usernames are stored; the data file holds thread and post links only.

Questions about LinkedIn Ads for small business

Do LinkedIn Ads work for small business?

In the 37 first-hand reports we found, 13 said LinkedIn Ads worked, 4 had mixed results, 15 said they did not work and 5 could not tell. Of the 32 with a clear verdict, 13 (40.6%) said it worked. The sample is small and leans toward software founders.

Are LinkedIn Ads worth it for B2B SaaS?

Often not for low-priced SaaS. 18 of the 37 reports came from software sellers: 6 said it worked and 9 said it did not. The usual complaint was paid clicks that never became sign-ups. Founders with high-value deals or a narrow audience did better.

How much do LinkedIn Ads cost per lead?

Only 2 owners in our sample gave a cost per lead: $25 to $50 per meeting booked through Lead Gen Forms, and $50 to $200 per qualified lead. Reported clicks ranged from $2 to $10. These are single accounts from different years.

Why do LinkedIn Ads get clicks but no leads?

9 of the 15 owners who said LinkedIn failed described exactly this. Common threads in their stories were a cold audience sent to a sign-up page, visits that never showed up in their own logs, and tracking that did not match their own records. Check leads against your CRM or server logs.

Should a small business start with LinkedIn Ads or Google Ads?

In our view, Google Ads first if people search for what you sell. LinkedIn makes sense when the buyer is a specific job title, the deal is worth a lot, and you can wait for a sales cycle. 6 of the 11 owners who compared said LinkedIn did worse.
