Product photos that work in ads (not the ones from your shoot)

The photos from your expensive shoot are for your website. Feeds reward something almost opposite.

Media Puppies team · 22 July 2026 · Updated 15 October 2026

Product photography for ads is a different job from a catalogue shoot. Brands spend serious money on a shoot, then use those images as ads and wonder why performance is flat. Catalogue photography is built for a considered browse on a product page. A feed is a different environment with different rules.

What underperforms

  • Pure white background product shots. They read as a catalogue, so they read as an advertisement, so they get scrolled.
  • Perfectly styled flat lays. Beautiful and anonymous — they look like every other brand in the category.
  • Model shots with no context. A person holding your product in a studio tells the viewer nothing about their own life.
  • Heavy retouching. Especially in skincare and food, where it triggers immediate scepticism.

What product photography for ads needs in a feed

In a real home

Your product on an actual kitchen counter, in a real bathroom, on a normal table. Slightly imperfect lighting. It reads as someone's life rather than a campaign.

Mid-use

Hands doing the thing. Applying, pouring, assembling, wearing. Motion implies reality.

Scale and size

The most common post-purchase complaint in Indian ecommerce is "smaller than I expected". A photo next to a familiar object prevents returns and builds trust before the sale.

The unglamorous detail

The texture, the stitching, the ingredient list, the thickness. Close and unstyled. This is what a sceptical buyer wants to see.

The photo that looks like it was taken by a customer usually outperforms the one that cost forty thousand rupees.

Producing it cheaply

  1. A phone, near a window. Genuinely sufficient for most categories. Avoid direct sun and overhead tube light.
  2. Shoot vertical. Reels and Stories inventory is where the cheaper attention is, and cropping a horizontal photo wastes most of the frame.
  3. Volume over polish. Thirty usable frames from one afternoon beats six perfect ones. You need angles to test.
  4. Ask customers. A small incentive for a photo of the product at home gives you imagery no studio can fake — and usage rights to run it as paid creative.

Where the good photos still belong

Your website, your product pages, your catalogue feed for shopping ads, your marketplace listings. Those are considered-browse environments where polish signals quality rather than advertising.

Two libraries, two jobs. The mistake is using one for both.

One test worth running

Take your best-performing ad and remake it twice: once with studio imagery, once shot on a phone in a real room. Same copy, same offer, same audience. Most Indian D2C accounts we've run this in see the phone version win — and it tells you where to spend production budget for the next year.

Questions people ask

Why do catalogue photos underperform as ads?

They are built for someone who has already arrived on a product page and chosen to look. A feed image has to earn attention from someone who did not ask, which usually means scale, context and a visible benefit rather than a clean white background.

Do I need a studio?

No. Daylight near a window, a plain surface and a recent phone cover most of what feeds reward. Spend the budget on more setups and more angles rather than on one expensive day.

How many images should an ad set have?

Three to five genuinely different images per concept — in use, in hand, in context, and one with the key number on it. Variations of the same shot mostly tell you which crop won.

Where do the polished catalogue photos still belong?

On the product page, in the gallery and in dynamic catalogue ads, where the viewer is already comparing and wants a clean, accurate look at the item. The two jobs are different, not in competition.
