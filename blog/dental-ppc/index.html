Dental PPC: how to run Google Ads that bring in new patients

A dental practice does not need more clicks. It needs new patients who book, show up and accept treatment, and PPC can be built to count exactly that.

Media Puppies · 27 October 2026

Dental PPC is pay-per-click advertising for dental practices, mostly Google Search ads, plus Local Services Ads where available and some remarketing within healthcare ad rules. The practice pays per click and the goal is new patients: emergency appointments, check-ups and, above all, consultations for high-value treatments like implants and clear aligners.

Dental PPC works when campaigns are split by treatment, each one sends traffic to a page built for that treatment, and tracking follows the lead from click to booked appointment to accepted treatment. Practices that run one campaign for "dentist near me" and measure clicks usually conclude PPC is expensive. Practices that measure cost per new patient by treatment usually keep spending.

How dental PPC works

Most dental PPC budgets go to Google Search, because people looking for a dentist search for one. A typical account has:

  • Search campaigns by treatment. Implants, clear aligners, emergency dentistry, cosmetic work such as veneers and whitening, general and family dentistry.
  • A brand campaign. The practice name, kept separate and cheap.
  • Local Services Ads where Google offers them to dentists, which charge per lead rather than per click. Availability varies by market, so check the sign-up page for your area.
  • A Google Business Profile linked to the account, so location assets show and Maps placements can appear. The profile itself matters as much as the ads; see Google Business Profile tips.
  • Limited remarketing through YouTube or Display, only within the healthcare rules covered below.

Meta ads can support implant and cosmetic campaigns, but Search is the starting point because it catches people who already want treatment.

Every practice has a different mix of capacity and margin. A useful way to decide is to look at intent, value and how each lead converts.

Keyword strategy and negatives for dental PPC

  • Treatment plus location. "Dental implants [city]", "emergency dentist [suburb]", "invisalign provider near me". These are the core.
  • Problem terms. "Broken tooth", "tooth pain at night", "missing teeth options". Good for emergency and implant campaigns.
  • Comparison terms. "Implants vs bridge", "aligners vs braces". Lower intent, useful for implant and aligner campaigns with content-rich pages.
  • Brand names of treatments. Terms such as Invisalign are trademarks. They can usually be bid on as keywords, but check the brand owner's provider rules and Google's trademark policy before using them in ad text.
  • Negatives. "Jobs", "dental assistant", "hygienist school", "salary", "dental school", "free clinic" (if you are not one), "diy", "kit", "at home", "insurance company", "medicaid" or "NHS" if you do not accept it, and other practice names you do not want to bid on.

Review the search terms report weekly for the first two months. Dental searches attract job seekers, students and people looking for at-home products, and the waste builds quickly. Our negative keywords audit explains the routine.

Healthcare ad policies and patient privacy

Dental ads sit inside healthcare advertising rules on every platform, and these shape what you can target and track.

  • Personalised advertising. Google's personalised advertising policy restricts targeting people based on health conditions. Remarketing lists built from visitors to treatment pages, and some audience targeting, can be limited or disapproved. Plan for Search to do most of the work.
  • Meta. Meta restricts ads that imply knowledge of a person's health ("Struggling with missing teeth?") and may limit the events a health-related site can send through the pixel.
  • Patient privacy in the US. HIPAA and federal guidance on online tracking technologies mean practices should be careful about what a tracking pixel can see. Keep patient details and appointment content out of tags, and ask your compliance adviser before tracking pages behind a patient login.
  • Before-and-after images. Allowed on some platforms and in some countries with conditions, restricted in others. Check each platform's policy and your national rules.

None of this makes dental PPC impractical. It means the account leans on search intent and clean conversion tracking, which is where most dental profit comes from anyway.

Tracking calls, forms and booked new patients

A dental PPC report that stops at "conversions" is incomplete. The chain you need is click, call or form, booked appointment, attended appointment, treatment accepted.

  1. Call tracking. Dynamic number insertion on the website, plus Google call assets, with a minimum call length so short calls do not count. Front desk call handling decides more than ad copy does, so listen to recordings; why leads don't answer calls covers the reverse problem of callbacks.
  2. Online booking and forms. Track each as its own conversion. If the booking widget is a third-party tool, confirm the completed booking fires a conversion and not just the widget opening.
  3. New vs existing patients. Existing patients often click the ad to find the phone number. Ask "Have you visited us before?" on calls and forms, and tag them.
  4. Treatment acceptance. Import accepted high-value treatment plans back into Google Ads as offline conversions where your practice management software allows it, without sending clinical details.

A worked example: cost per new patient

These numbers are hypothetical. Say a practice in Melbourne or Dallas spends $6,000 a month on dental PPC: $4,000 on implants and $2,000 on emergency and general dentistry.

The two campaigns cannot be compared on cost per click or cost per lead. Implants produce fewer, far more valuable results, and the practice should judge them on gross profit per accepted plan. The general campaign looks cheap per patient, and its real value depends on how many of those patients stay for years. If the front desk converted 80% of implant requests into attended consultations instead of 67%, the implant campaign would produce more accepted plans with no change in ad spend.

Landing pages that turn clicks into consultations

  • One page per treatment. Implant searchers should land on an implant page, never the home page.
  • Answer the real questions. How long it takes, how many visits, whether it hurts, what financing exists, who does the procedure.
  • Show the clinician. Name, photo, qualifications and professional registration build more trust than stock images.
  • Make booking easy. A click-to-call button on mobile, a short form, and online booking where possible.
  • Reviews within the rules. Use them where your national rules allow, and avoid clinical testimonials where they do not (see Australia below).

Ad copy that passes review and still converts

Dental ads get disapproved or underperform for predictable reasons. A few rules keep them both compliant and specific.

  • Describe the service, not the reader. "Dental implants in Brisbane, consult with Dr Lee" is fine. "Embarrassed by your missing teeth?" implies knowledge of the reader's health and is likely to be restricted.
  • Be precise about offers. If you advertise a free consultation or a new-patient exam, say exactly what it includes and state the terms on the landing page. Vague offers attract the wrong patients and can breach national rules.
  • Use the clinician and the location. Practitioner names, years of practice, suburb or neighbourhood, parking and opening hours all lift click-through rates on local searches.
  • Avoid outcome promises. "Pain-free", "guaranteed results" and "best dentist in town" can break advertising rules for health services in the UK, Canada and Australia, and invite complaints in the US.
  • Fill the assets. Call assets for emergency campaigns, location assets from the Business Profile, sitelinks to each treatment page and to finance information, and callouts for insurance, health funds or payment plans accepted.

Test two or three angles per treatment, such as speed, clinician expertise and finance, and judge them on booked consultations rather than click-through rate.

Budgeting dental PPC across treatments and locations

Budget follows capacity first. If the implant surgeon has two consultation slots a week, the implant campaign only needs enough budget to fill them with a margin for no-shows. Spending more produces a waiting list and wasted leads.

  • Give each treatment its own budget so a cheap campaign cannot drain an expensive one.
  • Multi-location groups should run a campaign per location or per cluster of nearby locations, with radius targeting matched to how far patients will travel. Otherwise the busiest location takes the budget.
  • Watch the calendar. In the US, many patients use insurance benefits before they reset at year end, which can lift demand late in the year. School holidays bring family appointments. Most practices close for some public holidays, so emergency campaigns need their schedules adjusted.
  • Review monthly on cost per accepted plan or new patient, and move budget toward the treatments with spare capacity and the best return.

Dental PPC in the US, UK, Canada and Australia

  • United States. Insurance and payment plans are a major search factor ("dentist that accepts [plan]"). State dental boards set advertising rules, including how specialties can be described. Lead forms that lead to calls or texts need clear consent language; see US lead form consent language.
  • United Kingdom. Many patients search for private dentists because NHS appointments are hard to find, so "private dentist [town]" and "emergency dentist" are strong terms. Ads must follow the CAP Code and the General Dental Council's guidance on ethical advertising.
  • Canada. Provincial dental regulators set advertising standards, which can cover testimonials, comparative claims and the use of "specialist". Check your province's rules before writing ad copy.
  • Australia. Dental advertising falls under the Health Practitioner Regulation National Law and AHPRA's advertising guidelines. Testimonials about clinical aspects of care are not allowed in advertising, and offers or discounts need their terms stated clearly. Patients also search by health fund, so mention the funds you work with.

Common dental PPC mistakes

  • One campaign for all treatments. Budget flows to cheap general clicks and implant searches go unserved.
  • Counting existing patients as new. Brand and general campaigns attract existing patients looking for the phone number.
  • No negatives for jobs and training. Dental assistant and hygienist searches are common and expensive.
  • Health-implying ad copy. Copy that suggests you know the reader's condition triggers disapprovals.
  • Ads running when nobody answers. Emergency ads after hours need an answering service or a schedule.
  • Judging implant campaigns too soon. Consultations can take weeks to become accepted plans; look at two to three months of data.

Managing dental PPC in-house or with an agency

A practice manager can run a small dental PPC account with a few hours a week, especially for emergency and general campaigns. Implant and aligner campaigns, offline conversion tracking and multi-location accounts usually need a specialist. When you speak to agencies, ask how they will separate new from existing patients, what they will report beyond clicks and calls, and how they handle healthcare ad policies in your country. Questions people ask before hiring

What is dental PPC?

Pay-per-click advertising for dental practices, mainly Google Search ads split by treatment, with the goal of booked new patients and accepted treatment plans.

Is Google Ads worth it for a dental practice?

It usually is when campaigns are split by treatment, calls are answered well and results are measured as cost per new patient or accepted plan, not cost per click.

Which dental treatments should I advertise first?

Most practices start with emergency dentistry and one high-value treatment such as implants, then add aligners or cosmetic work once tracking shows what each returns.

Can dental practices use remarketing?

Only in limited ways. Google and Meta restrict targeting based on health information, so remarketing to visitors of treatment pages can be limited or disapproved.

Can I use Invisalign in my Google Ads?

You can usually bid on it as a keyword. Using the name in ad text depends on the brand owner’s provider rules and Google’s trademark policy, so check both first.

How do I track new patients from dental PPC?

Use call tracking with a minimum call length, track completed online bookings, ask whether callers are new patients, and import accepted treatment plans as offline conversions.

Can Australian dental ads use patient testimonials?

Not about clinical aspects of care. AHPRA’s advertising guidelines under the National Law prohibit those testimonials in advertising for regulated health services.

How long before dental PPC shows results?

Emergency calls can come within days. Implant and aligner campaigns need two to three months, because consultations take time to become accepted treatment plans.
