Full service marketing agency or specialists: how to choose, and what each gets wrong

The honest version of this argument is not that one is better. It is that both fail in a predictable way, and you should pick the failure you can manage.

Media Puppies · 15 October 2026 · Updated 27 October 2026

A full service marketing agency handles most or all of a company's marketing under one contract: strategy, paid advertising, SEO, content, social media, email, creative and often the website. Specialist agencies do one discipline, such as paid search, SEO or video, and do it in more depth. The choice between them is a trade-off between coordination and depth, and the right answer depends mainly on whether someone in your business has the time to coordinate several suppliers.

The full service pitch is coordination: one contract, one team, one report, nobody blaming anybody. The specialist pitch is depth: the person running your Google Ads does nothing but Google Ads and is better at it than a generalist can be. Both are true. What nobody says out loud is how each one fails, which is more useful to know before you sign. This guide covers what a full service marketing agency does, how each option fails, a worked example of both, how to choose, and the middle path most growing businesses end up on.

What is a full service marketing agency?

A full service marketing agency offers every major marketing discipline in-house, or through partners it manages for you. A typical list includes:

  • Marketing strategy and planning
  • Paid search and paid social advertising
  • SEO and content marketing
  • Social media management
  • Email marketing and automation
  • Branding, design and video
  • Website design and development
  • Analytics and reporting

The real benefit is not the length of that list. It is that one team can see the whole funnel, from the first ad impression to the sale.

What a full service agency actually gives you

When the cost per lead rises, somebody at a full service agency can check whether the landing page changed, whether the SEO team republished a page, or whether the email sequence broke, without four companies emailing each other. That matters more than it sounds. Many of the expensive problems in marketing live in the gaps between disciplines: ads sending traffic to a page the content team rewrote, tracking broken by a developer who was never told, a sale running on the site that the ad copy does not mention.

How it fails: breadth without depth. The agency is genuinely good at one or two things and adequate at the rest, and you cannot tell which from the outside. The adequate parts quietly underperform for a year, and because everything sits in one contract, fixing one weak discipline means renegotiating the whole relationship.

What specialist agencies actually give you

Depth, and the ability to replace one part without rebuilding everything. If the paid search is weak, you change the paid search agency. Specialists also tend to put senior people on accounts, use the best tools for their discipline, and keep up with platform changes faster, because that one discipline is their whole business.

How it fails: nobody owns the whole number. Each specialist reports a healthy picture of its own channel, and the business's actual cost per customer rises anyway. Nobody is wrong and nobody is responsible. The coordination a full service agency did quietly becomes your job: shared targets, one definition of a qualified lead, one place where the numbers are reconciled.

How to tell which trade-off you can afford

It comes down to whether you have someone in-house who can hold the whole picture.

If you have a marketing lead with the time and the authority to run specialists, specialists will usually produce better work. They need managing: briefs, shared targets, a single definition of a qualified lead, and one place where the numbers are reconciled. That is a real job, not a weekly call.

If the person buying marketing is the founder, and marketing is one of six things they do, a full service agency is usually the better bet, even if each discipline is a little weaker. The coordination has to happen somewhere, and it will not happen between four suppliers on its own.

A worked example

Say a UK software company with a two-person marketing team spends 20,000 pounds a month across Google Ads, LinkedIn and SEO. These numbers are hypothetical.

Option one: a full service agency runs everything. Reporting is simple and the marketing team spends a few hours a week on the relationship. After six months, paid search performs well but SEO has barely moved, and the agency's SEO team is junior.

Option two: a paid media specialist and an SEO specialist. Each channel improves faster, but the marketing manager now spends a day a week aligning targets, reconciling two reports and settling which agency gets credit for leads that touched both channels.

Neither option is wrong. The first suits a team without time to manage suppliers; the second suits a team that has a marketing manager whose job includes that coordination. The mistake is choosing specialists without budgeting the time to run them.

Full service versus specialists for ecommerce and B2B

The right answer shifts by business model.

Ecommerce brands live and die by paid media, product feeds, email and conversion rate. These are tightly linked: a feed problem shows up in Shopping ads, an email flow affects repeat purchase, a site change affects every channel's return. A strong paid media agency that also owns tracking, with email and creative handled by specialists or in-house, is a common and effective setup. A full service agency suits brands early in their growth that need one team to set everything up.

B2B companies have long sales cycles, small audiences and a sales team that decides which leads are good. Paid search, LinkedIn and content must all feed the same CRM and the same definition of a qualified lead. Specialists can work well here if marketing operations is strong in-house. Without that, the reporting quickly fragments and nobody can see cost per opportunity. B2B PPC covers the paid side.

The middle path most businesses end up on

One agency owns paid media and measurement, because those two are inseparable, and specialists handle anything genuinely niche: technical SEO on a large site, PR, a creative studio for brand work.

That split works because it draws the line where the dependency actually is. Ads, landing pages, tracking and reporting are one system; splitting them across suppliers produces arguments about whose number is right. Brand film and media buying are not one system, and nobody suffers if they live in different companies.

Full service agency versus an in-house marketing team

A third option is to build the team yourself. An in-house team knows the business, the customers and the product better than any agency, and it can move fast on small changes. Its limits are breadth and cover: a team of two or three cannot be expert in paid search, SEO, email, design and analytics at once, and holidays or resignations leave gaps.

Many growing businesses combine the two. An in-house marketing lead owns strategy, the brand and the overall number, and agencies or specialists handle execution where depth matters most, usually paid media and technical SEO. As the business grows, more disciplines move in-house once there is enough work to keep a specialist busy all year. The agency vs in-house calculator helps find that point for each discipline.

Whichever mix you choose, the rule from earlier still applies: someone has to own the whole number. With an in-house lead, that person is obvious. Without one, a full service agency or a single lead agency has to play that role, and the contract should say so.

What a full service marketing agency costs

Full service agencies usually charge a monthly retainer that covers an agreed scope across disciplines, sometimes with project fees for websites or campaigns. Specialists charge per discipline. Compare the total, including your own team's time spent coordinating, rather than fee against fee. A cheaper set of specialists can cost more once a manager's day a week is counted. What to ask a full service marketing agency

  • Which disciplines do you deliver in-house, and which through partners?
  • Who leads our account, and who does the day-to-day work in each discipline?
  • Which two or three disciplines are you strongest in? Can you show results in each?
  • How will you report one overall number, such as cost per customer, across channels?
  • Can we reduce or remove one discipline without renegotiating the whole contract?
  • Will every account, analytics property and creative file be in our name?

The third and fifth questions are the most revealing. An agency that claims equal strength in everything, or that cannot unbundle a service, is telling you how hard it will be to fix a weak area later.

How to run specialists well

  • One shared target, such as cost per qualified lead or cost per customer, that every specialist reports against.
  • One definition of a qualified lead, agreed with sales and given to every supplier.
  • One source of truth for results, usually your CRM or analytics, not each agency's dashboard.
  • A monthly joint review where suppliers see each other's numbers and plans.
  • Clear ownership of shared assets such as landing pages and tracking, written down.

Common mistakes when choosing

  • Choosing specialists without budgeting coordination time. The work does not disappear; it moves to you.
  • Choosing full service for the breadth on the website. Breadth is cheap to claim. Ask which disciplines are genuinely strong.
  • Splitting paid media and tracking across suppliers. This is the split that most often produces arguments about whose numbers are right.
  • Letting each supplier define success differently. One target, one definition of a qualified lead, one source of truth.
  • Locking into a long contract before seeing results. Month-to-month or a short initial term keeps either setup honest.

How to tell whether your setup is working

Whatever you choose, judge it on one number the business cares about, usually cost per customer or cost per qualified lead, measured in your own CRM or analytics rather than in each agency's dashboard. Look at the trend over a quarter, not a month. If the overall number improves, the setup is working, even if one channel looks weaker on its own. If each channel reports progress and the overall number gets worse, the coordination is failing, whoever is responsible for it.

A simple decision rule

If you remember one thing from this guide, make it this. Ask who in your business will spend at least half a day a week aligning marketing suppliers, reconciling their numbers and holding them to one target. If you can name that person and they have the time, specialists or a hybrid will usually give you better work. If you cannot name anyone, choose a full service agency, or one lead agency that owns paid media and measurement, and make overall results its explicit responsibility in the contract.

Signs it is time to switch

Move from full service to specialists when one discipline clearly underperforms and the agency cannot fix it, or when you hire a marketing lead who can coordinate suppliers. Move from specialists to full service, or to the hybrid model, when coordination is eating your week, reports never agree, or nobody can say what a customer actually costs. Whichever direction you move, make sure every ad account, analytics property and creative file is in your name first. Leaving an agency cleanly covers the handover.

Choosing for US, UK and Australian businesses

The trade-off is the same in every market. Two practical points differ. Time zones matter more when specialists sit in several countries, so agree overlap hours for the suppliers you depend on most. And regulated categories such as finance and healthcare need every supplier to know the local advertising rules, which is easier to check with one agency than with five.

How to tell which model you are really being sold

  • Which of these services do you do in-house, and which do you subcontract? Many full service agencies are a coordination layer over the same specialists you could hire directly.
  • Who is the single person accountable for cost per customer? If the answer is a committee, it is nobody.
  • Show me a report that covers all the channels together. Agencies that genuinely work across disciplines have one. Agencies that staple four reports together will send you four reports.
  • What do you do badly? The ones worth hiring answer this.

Questions people ask before hiring

What is a full service marketing agency?

An agency that handles most or all marketing under one contract: strategy, paid ads, SEO, content, social media, email, creative and often web development.

Is a full service marketing agency more expensive?

Often, because you pay for a broad team and the coordination between disciplines. Specialists can cost less per channel, but you or a manager then spend time coordinating them.

Can I switch from a full service agency to specialists?

Yes. Make sure every account, analytics property and creative file is in your name first, then move one channel at a time so results stay comparable.

Is a full service marketing agency better than specialists?

Neither is better in general. A full service agency buys coordination and tends to be uneven across disciplines. Specialists buy depth and leave the coordination to you. The right answer depends on whether anyone in-house can hold the whole picture.

What does a full service agency actually fail at?

Depth. Most are genuinely strong in one or two areas and adequate in the rest, and from outside you cannot tell which is which until a channel has underperformed for several months.

What goes wrong with using several specialists?

Nobody owns the overall number. Each agency reports a healthy view of its own channel while the blended cost per customer rises, and the weekly coordination an agency used to absorb quietly becomes your job.

What is a sensible middle path?

Keep ads, landing pages, tracking and reporting with one team, because they are one system, and use specialists for genuinely separate work such as PR, brand film or heavy technical SEO.
