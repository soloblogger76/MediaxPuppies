Google Ads account suspended: what 162 advertisers report about why, the appeal and how long it took

We read public forum posts by advertisers whose own Google Ads account was suspended and coded 162 of them. Most never said how it ended. Of those who did, fewer than a third got back in, and the ones who reached a person at Google did far better than the ones who only met forms.

Key findings

  • We coded 162 first-hand reports of a suspended Google Ads account (90 on Hacker News, 72 on the Shopify Community, written from 2010 to 2026). 17 got the account back, 40 stayed suspended and 105 never said how it ended.
  • Of the 57 with a known ending, 17 (29.8%) were reinstated. On Hacker News that share fell from 10 of 22 in reports up to 2015 to 1 of 14 in reports from 2021 to 2026.
  • Google named a policy in 101 reports (62.3%). Misrepresentation led with 45, nearly all from store owners, then circumventing systems with 17, of which 16 were written from 2021 on. 17 owners said Google gave no reason at all.
  • Reaching a person made the biggest difference. 9 of the 21 owners who reached someone at Google and reported an ending got back in (42.9%), against 1 of the 14 (7.1%) who said they could not reach anyone.
  • Only 7 reports gave a time to resolution: reinstated after 1, 14, 30, 30, 30 and 150 days, and one final refusal after 30 days. 9 owners whose cases were still open had already waited from 7 to 150 days.

When a Google Ads account is suspended, the advertisers who write about it in public mostly never come back to say how it ended. In the 162 first-hand reports we coded, 17 got the account back, 40 stayed suspended and 105 (64.8%) left the story open. Of the 57 with a known ending, 17 (29.8%) were reinstated.

The reports also show which suspensions tend to end well. A Google Ads account suspended for payment or billing problems, or one the owner believed was a plain mistake, came back more often than one tied to affiliate offers, a restricted product or a second account. The biggest single difference was whether the advertiser reached a person at Google. None of our own client or account data is in here, and our opinions are labelled.

Who wrote these 162 reports

On 26 November 2026 we searched two public forums that load without a login: Hacker News and the Shopify Community. Hacker News gave us 90 reports, mostly founders and developers, running from 2010 to 2026. The Shopify Community gave us 72, almost all store owners writing since 2021. Each report is one advertiser describing their own account. Agencies describing client accounts, vendors offering to fix suspensions and Google staff are left out. 13 repeat posts were merged, so each person counts once.

Online stores and product makers wrote 77 of the reports, software, app and game makers 21, affiliate sites 10 and service businesses 3. The other 51 never said what they sold. Only 18 named a country: the UK 8 times, the US 7, Australia 2 and Canada 1.

We set out to count reasons and outcomes. We expected long waits. We did not expect how rarely an ending gets posted at all, or how sharply the odds changed with a human contact.

Google Ads account suspended: the reasons Google gave

In 101 of the 162 reports (62.3%) the advertiser quoted a named policy. 17 said Google gave no reason at all, and 44 did not say what the notice said. The two sources describe different worlds. Misrepresentation, the policy about how honestly a business presents itself, appears 45 times, and 43 of those are Shopify store owners. Circumventing systems, the policy Google uses when it believes an advertiser is evading its review, appears 17 times, and 16 of those reports were written from 2021 on.

Two smaller reasons, an unpaid balance and advertiser verification, came up once each. The outcome columns count only reports with a known ending, so most rows are small. Treat them as a direction only.

Circumventing systems. This was the reason that confused people most. A SaaS founder suspended a day after sign-up wrote: "I haven't even started a campaign yet, not sure what I could be 'circumventing'." Others had a dormant account flagged after months untouched, or a site behind a bot-blocking service that stopped Google's own crawler from loading the page. Only 1 of the 5 circumventing cases with a known ending was reinstated.

Suspicious payments. These were often triggered by an ordinary change: a new card, a billing address that no longer matched, a failed charge years earlier or a payments profile left behind after an account move. Counting every report where Google or the owner pointed at payments, there were 15, and 11 of them date from 2021 on.

No reason at all. 16 of the 17 reports with no reason given came from Hacker News. One founder summed up the support replies he got: "We've reviewed your case and can confirm you violated the policies. We can't tell you how though."

A few patterns hold up across the small numbers. Owners who believed it was a simple mistake got back in most often: 5 of the 7 with a known ending. Affiliate sites did worst. None of the 8 affiliate sites with a known ending got its account back, and several older reports describe a ban arriving months or years after a short affiliate test was paused. Restricted products and second accounts went the same way: 0 of 4 and 0 of 3.

The old-ads group is the strangest. 13 advertisers blamed ads they had paused or deleted long ago, often pointing at a domain that had since expired. In one 2012 case, a large advertiser of 5 years argued with Google for about a month over an ad deleted 4 years earlier, and was reinstated within days once the old destination worked again.

Three appeal paths and where each one ended

116 of the 162 advertisers said they appealed or asked for a review, and 16 of the 17 who got back in had appealed. Appealing on its own decided little: 16 of the 48 appellants with a known ending were reinstated (33.3%). What separated the outcomes was who answered.

9 of the 17 reinstatements came after the advertiser reached someone. A 2011 advertiser with a five-year-old account spending $30,000 a year made polite phone calls and was back in about two weeks, explaining that "they can escalate the matter internally so that a human reviews your suspension." Reaching a person was no guarantee, though: 12 people who did still stayed suspended, usually after being told the decision was final.

The forms-only path produced one reinstatement in 14 known endings. Owners on that path described appeals rejected within seconds, the same pasted reply to every message and, for some Shopify store owners, running out of review requests altogether.

Days to reinstatement, where anyone said

Only 7 of the 162 reports gave a time from suspension to a final answer, so this is a timeline of single cases. The fastest reinstatement came after 1 day, for a store owner suspended for circumventing systems who had removed an ads app and appealed. The slowest came after 150 days, for a store owner whose ads and Merchant Center accounts had been held for misrepresentation: "So, after 5 months, my Google Merchant Center Account and my Google Shopping Ads Account are finally unsuspended!" Three reinstatements took about 30 days. A US manufacturer that added protective equipment to its line in 2020 waited 30 days for its appeal, and the answer was a final no.

The open cases show what waiting looks like. 9 owners still waiting said how long they had waited: 7, 30, 30, 32, 60, 60, 90, 120 and 150 days. A side-business owner suspended for suspicious payments, who had filed the appeal Google's notice asked for, wrote: "Now it has been 22 business days by my count. There has never been any response."

We expected a long tail. The surprise was how few writers ever posted the end of the story, which in our view says more about the experience than any single number here.

When Merchant Center took the ads down

We coded Merchant Center suspensions as a separate category, and counted one only when the writer said it stopped their Google Ads or Shopping ads. 46 reports fit, nearly all from Shopify store owners. Their cases stay open longest: 2 were reinstated, 5 stayed suspended and 39 (84.8%) never said how it ended.

The pattern in these threads is a chain. The Merchant Center account is suspended, usually for misrepresentation, and the linked Google Ads account is held until the store passes review. One owner relayed what Google told them: "My Google Ads account can't be reinstated until the linked Merchant Center issues are resolved." Owners in this group most often blamed store content: a missing postcode in the business address, reviews imported from another marketplace, placeholder text left after a theme change, or product data that did not match the page. Several store owners also said Merchant Center cleared first while the ads account stayed suspended, so the two need to be checked separately.

The older stories are the AdWords era: affiliate tests, old ads and phone calls to a rep who could escalate. Most of the reinstatements came from that period. The recent ones are mostly brand-new accounts, circumventing systems and payment flags, with few people reaching anyone. With 14 known endings in the latest period, treat this as a direction. It fits what advertisers describe about support moving to forms.

A long spending history helped a little. 19 reports mentioned spend or account age, from a few hundred dollars in total to $30,000 a month. 3 of the 7 with a known ending got back in (42.9%), against 14 of 50 (28.0%) among those who mentioned neither. One small online brand that had spent $30,000 a month for 3 years was still banned for opening a second account.

What the reports suggest you check before appealing

These checks come only from what went wrong, or right, in the 162 reports. Each one is about finding a real problem and fixing it before you ask Google to look again. Appeal through the link in the suspension notice in your Google Ads account, and read Google's own Google Ads Help pages on suspensions for the current process.

  1. Read the exact policy named in the notice. 101 advertisers were given one. Every reinstated story we read answered that one policy directly in the appeal.
  2. Check billing before anything else. 15 reports traced back to payments: a card swapped without updating the billing address, a failed charge, a chargeback or a payments profile tied to an old login. One team got back in once they submitted the payments profile ID shown in the ads account itself.
  3. Look for old paused ads and dead domains. 13 advertisers blamed ads paused long ago, often pointing to a domain that had expired. Check every final URL in the account, paused ones included, still loads your own page.
  4. If you sell through Merchant Center, fix the store first. In the 46 Merchant Center reports, the ads account was held until the store passed review. Check the business name and full address match everywhere, remove imported reviews and placeholder text, and make product data match the page.
  5. Do not open a second account. Several notices quoted in these threads warn that new accounts will be suspended too, and none of the 3 advertisers with a second or linked account and a known ending got back in.
  6. Try to reach a person, politely. 9 of 21 who reached someone got back in, against 1 of 14 who did not. Use the support contact in your account and any account manager you already have, keep records, and answer what was asked.
  7. Use your reviews carefully. Some store owners used up their review requests on half-fixed sites. Fix, check, then ask.

In our view: what this means for an advertiser

In our view, the most useful thing these 162 people tell you is that a suspension is mostly a records problem. The reinstated advertisers could show that their payments, pages, products and history matched what Google expected, and someone at Google looked. The ones who stayed out were usually running affiliate offers, a restricted product or a second account, or never reached anyone at all.

The other lesson is about dependence. Several owners here described losing a large share of their customers the week the account went dark, and our Google Ads owner study shows how much small advertisers expect from the channel once it runs. In our view, a business with real search demand should keep its account records clean as a matter of routine, and keep at least one other channel warm.

Scope

This study follows the method of our owner studies of Google Ads, Facebook ads, Microsoft Ads, LinkedIn Ads, Performance Max, Reddit Ads and TikTok ads from 21 to 25 November 2026. We used two public sources that load without a login: Hacker News, through its public search API, and the Shopify Community, through its public JSON. We did not work around any block, CAPTCHA or login, and used no paid tools.

On Hacker News we ran 45 comment searches, such as "adwords suspended", "google ads account suspended", "circumventing systems", "suspicious payment activity" and "google ads appeal", and kept the 4,751 comments they returned. Two text filters cut those to 260 that name Google Ads or AdWords near a suspension word and a first-person phrase, and we read all 260. We also searched 379 Hacker News posts and read 21 suspension threads in full. On the Shopify Community we ran 27 searches, found 611 topics, downloaded 469 of them and screened the 398 whose first post names a suspension word. Some search pages returned a rate-limit error and were not retried.

A report counts when the writer describes a suspension of their own Google Ads account, as the owner, founder or in-house marketer. Merchant Center suspensions count only when the writer says their Google Ads or Shopping ads were stopped by them; Merchant Center problems on stores that ran no ads were left out. We excluded agencies, freelancers and vendors describing client accounts or offering help, Google staff, AdSense publishers, non-English threads, and anyone identifiably outside the US, UK, Canada and Australia. Where one person posted several times we counted the person once: 13 repeat posts were merged. Each report was coded once, by one person.

Limits. Everything is self-reported, unchecked and one side of the story. People post when they are stuck and rarely come back with an update, so 105 of the 162 reports never say how the case ended. Hacker News leans toward software founders and older AdWords stories, and the Shopify Community toward new stores and Merchant Center misrepresentation, so comparisons between periods mix two different crowds; we show the period comparison for Hacker News alone. 144 of the 162 reports give no country and only 7 give a time to resolution. No usernames are stored; the data file holds thread and post links only.

Questions about Google Ads suspensions

Why was my Google Ads account suspended?

In the 162 reports we coded, the most common reasons Google named were misrepresentation (45 reports, nearly all online stores), circumventing systems (17) and suspicious payments or billing (9). 17 advertisers said Google gave no reason. Read the exact policy in your suspension notice first.

Can a suspended Google Ads account be reinstated?

Yes, sometimes. Of the 57 reports with a known ending, 17 (29.8%) were reinstated and 40 stayed suspended. Reinstatement was far more common when the advertiser reached a person at Google: 9 of 21, against 1 of 14 who could not.

How long does a Google Ads suspension appeal take?

Only 7 reports said. Reinstatements came after 1, 14, 30, 30, 30 and 150 days, and one appeal was refused after 30 days. Owners still waiting had waited from 7 to 150 days.

What does circumventing systems mean for a Google Ads suspension?

It is the policy Google uses when it believes an advertiser is trying to get around its review. 17 advertisers in our sample were suspended under it, 16 of them from 2021 on, often on new or dormant accounts. Only 1 of the 5 with a known ending was reinstated.

Should I open a new Google Ads account after a suspension?

No. Notices quoted in these threads say new accounts will be suspended too, and none of the 3 advertisers in our sample with a second or linked account and a known ending got back in. Appeal through the link in your suspension notice instead.
