Form design: every field costs you completions

The form is where you find out whether everything before it was worth doing. Most are built without anyone testing them on a phone.

Media Puppies · 20 September 2026 · Updated 15 October 2026

Lead form design is a negotiation. Every field asks for something in exchange for a promise you made earlier on the page. Ask for more than the promise is worth and people leave.

Lead form design starts with field count

Ask only what you need to make the first contact useful. For most Indian lead-gen that's name, phone, and one qualifying question. Everything else can be asked on the call, when they're already talking to you.

The exception is deliberate qualification — a budget field that filters. That field costs you completions on purpose, and it's worth it.

Mobile failures that produce zeros

  • Wrong input type. type="tel" for phone, type="email" for email — otherwise people get the wrong keyboard and give up.
  • Submit button under the browser bar. Test on a real device.
  • Dropdowns that don't open in some mobile browsers.
  • Date pickers fighting the operating system.

Any of these turns a page that looks fine on your laptop into a page that converts nobody.

Fill in your own form, on your own phone, on mobile data. Most people never have.

Errors

Show them inline, next to the field, as the person moves on — not as one message at the top after submit. And never clear what they typed.

After submit

A thank-you state that says what happens next and when. Plus an automated WhatsApp message that arrives before they've closed the tab. This is the cheapest conversion improvement available and almost nobody does it.

What doesn't matter

Button colour, corner radius, which side the labels sit on. At the traffic most Indian lead-gen businesses have, those changes are undetectable. Test big things instead.

The two-step form

Splitting one form across two screens reliably lifts completions, and the reason is behavioural rather than technical. The first screen asks something easy and low-commitment — a dropdown, a choice, a single tap. Having answered it, the visitor has made a small investment and is measurably more likely to finish.

The practical arrangement for lead generation: step one asks what they need and roughly what they are willing to spend, both as selects with no typing. Step two asks for name and phone. Put the qualifying questions first and the contact details second, never the other way around — a form that opens by demanding your phone number reads as a trap.

The bonus is measurement. If people complete step one and abandon at step two, you have a trust problem. If they never finish step one, the offer above the form is wrong. A single-screen form cannot tell you which.

What to do with the submit button

"Submit" is the weakest word available. The button should restate what the person gets: "Get my quote", "Send me the plan", "Book the site visit". It is a one-word edit with a measurable effect, and it costs nothing.

Then disable it on click and change the label to something like "Sending…". Without that, slow connections produce duplicate submissions, and a lead arriving three times is a lead your sales team annoys three times.

Validation that does not punish people

  • Validate on blur, not on every keystroke. Turning a field red while someone is still typing it is hostile.
  • Accept messy phone numbers. Spaces, +91, a leading zero — strip them server-side. Rejecting "98765 43210" loses real leads over formatting.
  • Never clear the form on error. Losing four filled fields because of one mistake is where people give up entirely.
  • Say what is wrong and how to fix it. "Enter a 10-digit mobile number" beats "Invalid input".

Test it the way a visitor experiences it

Fill in your own form, on your own phone, on mobile data rather than office wifi, and then wait to see what actually arrives. Most owners have never done this. It routinely uncovers a broken submit button on iOS, a notification email going to a mailbox nobody opens, or a thank-you page that never loads.

Questions people ask

How many fields should a lead form have?

As few as the next conversation needs. Every field costs completions, and every qualifying field trades volume for quality. Which trade is right depends on whether your sales team is short of leads or short of time.

What are the most common mobile form failures?

The wrong keyboard for the field, a submit button hidden behind the keyboard, errors that clear what was typed, and a page that scrolls the form out of view on focus. Each one produces a page that converts at close to zero and looks fine on a desktop.

Does a two-step form work better?

Often, because the first step asks for something easy and the second arrives after the person has committed. It is not universal — for short forms the extra step adds friction without the benefit.

What should happen after submit?

A clear confirmation that says what happens next and when, not a blank page or a generic thank you. It is also where a tracking event should fire, and where a WhatsApp link earns its place.
