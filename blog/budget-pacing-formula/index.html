Budget pacing: the formula, a spreadsheet layout, and when to step in

Monthly budgets get blown in the last week or left unspent in the first. A pacing sheet that takes five minutes a day stops both.

Budget pacing is the habit of checking, every day, whether an ad account is on track to spend its monthly budget by the last day of the month. It sounds administrative. It is the difference between a client who trusts the numbers and one who gets an invoice twice the size they expected, or discovers on the 28th that half the budget never went out.

Here is the budget pacing formula we use, the spreadsheet layout behind it, and the rules for when a gap is worth acting on.

The budget pacing formula

Three numbers, all calculated from the same date.

Expected spend to date = monthly budget × (days elapsed ÷ days in the month).

Pacing = actual spend to date ÷ expected spend to date. A result of 1.00 is perfectly on track; 1.15 means fifteen percent ahead; 0.85 means fifteen percent behind.

Daily budget needed to finish on target = (monthly budget − actual spend to date) ÷ days remaining.

That third number is the one you act on. If it is wildly different from the daily budget currently set in the platform, the account will either overshoot or fall short, and the earlier you adjust, the smaller the adjustment.

A five-column budget pacing spreadsheet

One row per campaign or per budget line, five columns, updated every morning:

  1. Monthly budget, as agreed with the client.
  2. Spend to date, pasted from the platform.
  3. Expected to date, using the formula above. In a sheet: =B2*(DAY(TODAY())-1)/DAY(EOMONTH(TODAY(),0)), counting completed days so a morning check is not unfairly behind.
  4. Pacing %: =C2/D2, with conditional formatting: green between 0.95 and 1.05, amber to 0.85 and 1.15, red beyond.
  5. Daily needed: =(B2-C2)/(DAY(EOMONTH(TODAY(),0))-DAY(TODAY())+1).

A total row at the bottom shows the account as a whole. Our Meta ads budget calculator does the planning side of this before the month starts.

How the platforms spend against a daily budget

Pacing gaps are partly caused by how the platforms treat daily budgets. Google Ads can spend up to twice the daily budget on a strong day, while capping the month at about 30.4 times the daily figure. Meta can spend up to three-quarters more than the daily budget on a given day, while keeping the calendar week at no more than seven times the daily budget.

So a single day at 160 percent is normal and corrects itself. A week at 120 percent is not the platform smoothing; it is a budget set too high for the month.

When to step in, and when to leave it

Ignore the first five days. Early-month pacing swings wildly because the denominator is small. Watch it, but do not change budgets on day three.

Act when the daily-needed figure differs from the set budget by more than about fifteen percent for three days running. Change it once, by the amount the formula says, not in nervous daily nudges.

Mind the learning phase. On Meta, a large budget change can reset learning; we covered the thresholds in the learning phase explained. Two moderate changes a week apart are usually safer than one large correction.

Underspend is not always a problem. If a campaign is behind because cost per result is rising and the buyer has deliberately held back, that is pacing doing its job. The sheet should have a notes column for exactly this, so the client sees a decision rather than a mistake.

Report pacing to the client every week

One line in the weekly report: spend to date, expected to date, and what will happen next. Clients who see pacing weekly do not get surprised by month-end, and month-end surprises are the most common reason agency relationships sour over things that had nothing to do with results.

Questions people ask before hiring

What is the formula for budget pacing?

Expected spend to date equals the monthly budget multiplied by days elapsed divided by days in the month. Pacing is actual spend divided by expected spend. The daily budget needed to finish on target is the remaining budget divided by the days remaining.

What is a good pacing percentage?

Between 95 and 105 percent is on track. Between 85 and 115 percent is worth watching. Beyond that, adjust the daily budget, unless the gap is a deliberate decision such as holding back spend while cost per result is rising.

Why does Google or Meta spend more than my daily budget?

Both platforms allow daily overspend on strong days and balance it over a longer period. Google can spend up to twice the daily budget in a day within a monthly cap; Meta can exceed it by up to three-quarters in a day within a weekly limit.

How often should I check pacing?

Every morning, in about five minutes, with a budget change only when the gap has persisted for a few days. Daily checking with infrequent changes is the habit that works.
