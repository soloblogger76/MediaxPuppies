The LTV to CAC ratio, calculated honestly

Almost every lifetime value figure in a pitch deck is wrong in the same three ways. Each one makes the business look better than it is.

Media Puppies · 11 October 2026

The LTV to CAC ratio — lifetime value divided by acquisition cost — is the standard shorthand for whether a business can afford to grow. It is a useful ratio. It is also the easiest number in marketing to inflate without technically lying, and most of the figures in circulation have been inflated in the same three ways.

Mistake one: using revenue instead of gross profit

A customer who spends a given amount over three years has not given you that amount. They have given you that amount minus the cost of making and delivering what they bought, minus payment processing, minus returns, minus the cost of serving them. For a business with thin margins the difference is most of the number.

Lifetime value should be gross profit. If your reported ratio looks healthy and your bank balance does not, this is usually why. The LTV calculator and the contribution margin calculator between them produce a figure finance would accept.

Mistake two: extrapolating retention you have not observed

A company two years old cannot know its five-year lifetime value. What it can do is take an early retention curve and project it forward, which is reasonable as a planning exercise and dangerous as a budgeting one — because churn is rarely flat, and the tail of a projection is doing most of the work.

Two disciplines help. Cap the horizon at something you have actually observed, or at a conservative multiple of it. And calculate by cohort rather than in aggregate, because an aggregate number blends your best early customers with your most recent ones and hides a deteriorating trend. Modelling retention properly changes the answer more than most people expect.

Mistake three: an acquisition cost that only counts media

Fully loaded acquisition cost includes the media, the people or agency running it, creative production, the tooling, and any sales cost involved in converting the lead. Dividing an inflated lifetime value by an understated acquisition cost is how a struggling business produces a ratio that looks like a success story.

Both halves of the ratio tend to be wrong in the same direction at once, which is why the error is usually larger than it looks.

What the LTV to CAC ratio does and does not tell you

A healthy ratio says each customer is worth meaningfully more than they cost, with enough margin to absorb being wrong. A ratio close to one says you are buying revenue rather than building a business. A very high ratio is not automatically good news either — it often means you are underspending and leaving volume to competitors who are willing to pay more for the same customer.

What it does not tell you is whether you can afford to grow this quarter. That is payback period, and the two numbers can point in opposite directions. Excellent lifetime value with a long payback is a financing problem, not a marketing one.

Using lifetime value to acquisition cost to set a media budget

Work backwards. Take gross-profit lifetime value, choose the ratio you want to run at, and divide. The result is your allowable fully loaded acquisition cost. Subtract the cost of the team or agency and the tooling, and what remains is what you can spend in the auction. Combine that with your close rate and you have a cost per lead ceiling derived from your own economics rather than a benchmark from someone else's.

That chain — lifetime value, allowable acquisition cost, cost per lead, bid — is how a media plan stops being a budget someone picked and becomes a consequence of the business model. It is also the fastest way to find out that the constraint is pricing or retention rather than advertising, which is a more useful discovery than a cheaper click.

How often to recalculate

Quarterly, and immediately after any price change, any change in the product mix, or any significant shift in where customers come from. Channels differ in the quality of customer they bring, and a ratio calculated across a blended mix can hide one channel acquiring customers who churn in month two.

If the numbers say your economics will not support paid acquisition yet, that is worth knowing before spending rather than after — whether a business is ready for paid ads is mostly this calculation. We ask for it before quoting, and the audit starts there.

Questions people ask before hiring

What is a healthy LTV to CAC ratio?

The commonly cited target is around three to one on gross profit, but the useful version is any ratio that leaves enough margin to absorb being wrong about retention. A ratio near one means you are buying revenue rather than profit.

Should lifetime value be based on revenue or profit?

Gross profit, after cost of goods, delivery, payment processing and returns. Revenue-based lifetime value overstates the ratio by exactly your cost of delivery, which for many businesses is most of it.

How far forward should I project lifetime value?

No further than you have observed, or a conservative multiple of it. A five-year projection from eighteen months of data is a planning assumption, not a number to set budgets against.

Can the ratio be too high?

Yes. A very high ratio usually means underspending — you are leaving volume to competitors willing to pay more for the same customer. It is a signal to test higher bids rather than a sign of discipline.

Does the ratio tell me how much I can spend this month?

No. It tells you whether a customer is worth acquiring. How many you can acquire before cash runs out is the payback period, and the two can point in opposite directions.
