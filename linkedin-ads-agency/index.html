LinkedIn

The most expensive click in B2B. Sometimes the cheapest customer.

LinkedIn charges a large multiple of what Google or Meta do for a click, and it is the only platform where you can reliably buy a specific job title at a specific company. Whether that trade is worth it comes down to one number: what a customer is worth to you.

The arithmetic

Work out whether it can pay before you spend anything.

This is a five-minute calculation and it should happen before the account is even opened. Most disappointing LinkedIn accounts were doomed at this step.

Work backwards from contract value

Take your average contract value, your gross margin and your close rate from qualified lead. That gives you what you can afford to pay for a qualified lead. Divide by a realistic landing page conversion rate and you have your ceiling on cost per click. Compare that to what LinkedIn actually charges in your category. If the ceiling is below the market rate, no amount of optimisation closes the gap — and any agency willing to take the retainer anyway is not on your side.

Where it consistently earns its price

Enterprise software, professional and financial services, recruitment for senior roles, industrial and capital equipment, and executive education. What these share is a large deal value and a buyer who is genuinely identifiable by title and firm.

Where it consistently does not

Small-contract SMB software, local services, anything consumer, and early-stage products still finding their buyer. In those cases Google search captures the intent that exists and Meta builds the demand, at a fraction of the cost.

Targeting

Buy the company, not the interest.

LinkedIn’s advantage is firmographic data that people maintain themselves because their careers depend on it. Using it like an interest-targeting platform wastes the one thing you are paying extra for.

Account lists beat job function

Uploading a target account list and layering seniority on top is far more precise than broad job-function targeting, and it lines up with how your sales team already thinks. If sales has a named account list, that is your audience — and if they do not, building one is more valuable than any campaign we could launch.

Company size and seniority filters that actually bind

Job titles are self-declared and chaotic. Seniority plus function plus company headcount is usually a tighter and more stable definition than a title list, which drifts as people rename their own roles.

Audience size and frequency

A very small audience on a real budget means the same few thousand people see your ad relentlessly. LinkedIn frequency caps are worth setting deliberately, and creative rotation matters more than the small audience size suggests.

Lead gen forms versus your own landing page

Native forms prefill from the profile and convert at high rates — with the usual consequence that some of those leads were barely paying attention. Sending traffic to your own page filters harder and costs more per lead. Which is right depends on whether your constraint is sales capacity or lead supply.

Measurement

LinkedIn is hardest to measure and easiest to under-credit.

Long sales cycles and a heavy dose of dark social mean the platform’s contribution rarely shows up cleanly in a last-click report.

Conversions API and offline imports

Wire up the server-side connection and push CRM stages back, the same way we do for Google and Meta. Without it, LinkedIn is judged on form fills and looks expensive; with it, it is judged on opportunities and often looks reasonable.

Ask people how they heard about you

For considered B2B purchases, a free-text "how did you hear about us" field on the form is frequently more informative than any attribution model. It is the cheapest measurement you will ever install.

Judge it over a sales cycle, not a month

If your cycle is three months, a one-month LinkedIn test tells you about cost per lead and nothing about value. Commit to a real window or do not start.

Straight answers.

Is LinkedIn really that much more expensive?

Yes, by a wide margin on a cost-per-click basis. The question is never whether the click is expensive — it always is — but whether the customer is cheap. For large contract values it frequently is.

Can we run LinkedIn on a small budget?

Badly. Small budgets on expensive clicks produce too few conversions to learn from and too little reach to register with an account list. It is better to run one tightly defined audience properly than four thinly.

Lead gen forms or landing pages?

Forms for volume and lower cost per lead, landing pages for quality and better qualification. We usually test both, and the right answer depends on whether your sales team is short of leads or short of time.

Do you do organic LinkedIn or executive ghostwriting?

No. We run paid media. Organic presence genuinely helps LinkedIn ads perform, and we will say so, but it is not a service we sell.

How long before we know if it works?

At least one full sales cycle. Anything shorter tells you about cost per lead and nothing about whether those leads become revenue.

Yes, and we do it more often on this platform than any other. The arithmetic on the first call usually settles it.
